1) Dynamic hedging as an economic relationship

We start with the hedge ratio, which tells us how much of another asset, or how much of a hedge basket, we need to short when we hold a target asset and want to reduce an exposure they share. The target is the asset we want to hedge, and the explanatory return comes from the hedge asset. We’ll estimate that ratio from their return relationship.

In the simplest one-hedge case, the statistical model is:

\[ y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + \varepsilon_t \]

where:

  • \(y_t\) is the target return at time \(t\),
  • \(x_t\) is the hedge return at time \(t\),
  • \(\alpha\) is the average return that isn’t explained by the hedge,
  • \(\beta\) is the hedge ratio,
  • \(\varepsilon_t\) is the residual, meaning the part of the target move that the hedge didn’t explain.

If \(\beta = 1.2\), the interpretation is direct: a 1% move in the hedge asset is associated with about a 1.2% move in the target. A hedged book then holds the target and shorts \(1.2\) units of the hedge:

\[ r^{hedged}_t = y_t - \beta x_t \]

The resulting return has a useful role in two related problems. In pure hedging, we want \(r^{hedged}_t\) to be less volatile than \(y_t\). In residual trading, we ask for something stronger: we want a residual spread to behave like a mean-reverting spread. We’ll work through hedging and residual trading as two connected layers.

The first layer is risk reduction:

How much of the target’s systematic exposure can we remove with the available hedge assets?

The second layer is residual trading:

After hedging, does the leftover spread mean-revert enough to trade?

A hedge can reduce risk very effectively while its residual still gives us no useful trading signal. Two returns can be highly correlated even when their price spread fails a stationarity test. We’ll study the risk of the hedged return first, and then examine the separate price-spread relationship for trading.

Regression and ordinary least squares

Before estimating hedge ratios, let’s build the regression model from the beginning. We observe a target return \(y_t\) and one or more possible hedge returns on the same trading day. Regression gives us a way to describe how the target tends to move with those hedge returns and to measure the part left over.

For a single hedge, we write:

\[ y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad t=1,\ldots,T \]

Here \(T\) is the number of paired observations in the estimation sample. The intercept \(\alpha\) is the model’s average target return when \(x_t=0\), and the slope \(\beta\) measures how much the conditional average of \(y_t\) changes for a one-unit change in \(x_t\). The error \(\varepsilon_t\) collects the part of the target return that the linear relationship doesn’t explain.

If the conditional mean of the error is zero, the model implies:

\[ E[y_t\mid x_t] = \alpha + \beta x_t, \qquad E[\varepsilon_t\mid x_t]=0 \]

The first equation is a statement about the average target return at a given hedge return. Actual daily observations will sit above and below that line because asset returns also respond to other factors and shocks. A linear association describes the co-movement in our sample; the slope alone doesn’t establish that a movement in one asset caused the movement in the other.

For returns expressed in the same units, \(\beta\) is a ratio of return changes. A positive slope means the target tends to move in the same direction as the hedge; a negative slope means it tends to move in the opposite direction. Its magnitude determines how much hedge exposure is associated with one unit of target exposure.

From observations to a fitted line

We don’t know \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) in advance, so we estimate them from the observed pairs \((x_t,y_t)\). For any candidate intercept \(a\) and slope \(b\), the fitted value and the fitted residual are:

\[ \hat y_t = a + bx_t, \qquad e_t = y_t-\hat y_t \]

The fitted value is the point on the candidate line at \(x_t\); the residual is the vertical distance from that line to the observed target return. The model error \(\varepsilon_t\) is an unobserved part of the data-generating relationship, while \(e_t\) is calculated after fitting a particular sample. We can inspect \(e_t\) directly, and we use it to assess the fit.

Ordinary least squares (OLS) chooses the intercept and slope that minimize the sum of squared residuals:

\[ (\hat\alpha,\hat\beta) = \arg\min_{a,b}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-a-bx_t)^2 \]

Squaring gives positive and negative errors the same treatment, and larger misses receive more weight. The sum uses every paired observation in the fitting window, so one unusually large daily move can have a visible influence on the fitted line. Once the coefficients are estimated, we write the predictions as \(\hat y_t=\hat\alpha+\hat\beta x_t\) and the residuals as \(\hat\varepsilon_t=y_t-\hat y_t\).

We can picture the procedure as moving and rotating a line through a scatter plot of the paired returns until its total squared vertical distance from the points is as small as possible. The result depends on the observations used in the fit, so a different sample can give us different coefficients.

Deriving the one-variable OLS coefficients

Write \(Q(a,b)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-a-bx_t)^2\). At the minimum, its derivatives with respect to the two coefficients are zero:

\[ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial a}=-2\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-a-bx_t)=0, \qquad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial b}=-2\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_t(y_t-a-bx_t)=0 \]

The first condition says that the fitted residuals sum to zero when we include an intercept. If \(\bar x=T^{-1}\sum_t x_t\) and \(\bar y=T^{-1}\sum_t y_t\), it gives:

\[ \hat\alpha = \bar y-\hat\beta\bar x \]

Substituting that intercept into the second condition gives the slope:

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_t-\bar x)(y_t-\bar y)} {\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_t-\bar x)^2} =\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(x,y)}{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(x)} \]

The numerator measures how the two returns move together around their sample averages. The denominator measures how much the hedge return varies around its own average. Their ratio scales the co-movement by the variation in the hedge. Since \(\hat\beta=\hat\rho_{xy}\hat\sigma_y/\hat\sigma_x\), holding correlation and target volatility fixed, a more volatile hedge requires fewer units for the same exposure.

The slope requires variation in \(x_t\); if every hedge return in the fitting sample were identical, the denominator would be zero and the slope couldn’t be estimated from that sample. With a fitted intercept, the line also passes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\), which follows directly from the intercept equation.

Multiple regression and the design matrix

Often a single asset can’t describe the exposures we want to remove. With \(K\) hedge returns, we can estimate their coefficients together:

\[ y_t=\alpha+\beta_1x_{1,t}+\beta_2x_{2,t}+\cdots+\beta_Kx_{K,t}+\varepsilon_t \]

Each \(\beta_j\) describes the fitted change associated with \(x_{j,t}\) while the other hedge returns are held fixed. For example, a stock can be regressed on a sector ETF and a broad market ETF together. The two coefficients then describe their contributions within the same fitted relationship, including any overlap between the hedge assets.

We arrange the observations in a design matrix with one row per date and one column per coefficient. The first column is all ones for the intercept:

\[ X=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{1,1} & \cdots & x_{K,1}\\ 1 & x_{1,2} & \cdots & x_{K,2}\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{1,T} & \cdots & x_{K,T} \end{bmatrix},\quad \boldsymbol\theta=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\\beta_1\\\vdots\\\beta_K\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf y=\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_T\end{bmatrix} \]

The same least-squares problem is now \(\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\lVert\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\theta\rVert^2\). Differentiating gives the normal equations and, when the columns of \(X\) are linearly independent, the familiar solution:

\[ X^\top X\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=X^\top\mathbf y, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y \]

Here \(X\) has \(T\) rows and \(K+1\) columns; the estimated vector contains one intercept and \(K\) hedge coefficients. In the implementation we use a least-squares solver, which finds the fit directly without explicitly forming the inverse. If hedge returns are very similar, their individual coefficients can be sensitive to small changes in the sample even when their combined fitted return is stable. Perfectly dependent columns don’t identify a unique set of individual coefficients.

OLS assumptions and coefficient estimates

The least-squares calculation always chooses coefficients to fit the observations it receives. To interpret those estimates as estimates of an underlying relationship, we also need assumptions about the observations and the errors. Written for the multiple-regression model \(\mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\theta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon\), the main assumptions have different roles:

  1. A linear conditional relationship. We model the conditional mean of the target as a linear combination of the included hedge returns and an intercept. The coefficients are linear even if we decide to include transformed explanatory variables.
  2. Enough independent information. The design matrix has full column rank, so no hedge column is an exact linear combination of the others. We also need more observations than fitted coefficients to estimate the residual variance below.
  3. Zero conditional mean. We assume \(E[\boldsymbol\varepsilon\mid X]=0\). Given the full set of hedge observations, the remaining error has no systematic average component. Under this assumption and full rank, the fitted coefficients are unbiased conditional on \(X\).
  4. Constant, uncorrelated errors for classical inference. The usual standard-error formula assumes the same error variance across observations and zero covariance between errors on different dates. These assumptions affect the usual variance calculation and efficiency result; they aren’t needed simply to calculate the OLS fit.
  5. Normal errors for exact small-sample tests. Joint normality of the errors conditional on \(X\), together with the classical conditions above, gives the familiar finite-sample \(t\) and \(F\) reference distributions. With financial returns, changes in volatility or dependence over time can call for a different estimate of coefficient uncertainty.

The zero-mean condition deserves attention. If an omitted factor moves the target and is correlated with a hedge return, some of that factor’s effect can be picked up by the fitted hedge coefficient. The coefficient still describes the fitted association in our sample, but interpreting it requires knowing which returns are included in the regression and which exposures are left in the error.

When the classical assumptions hold, OLS has the lowest coefficient variance among linear unbiased estimators, the Gauss–Markov result. Overlapping hedge assets can nevertheless make individual estimates imprecise: their shared variation makes it harder to separate their contributions to the fitted target return.

Residuals, fitted variation, and estimation uncertainty

With an intercept, the normal equations give \(X^\top\hat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=0\). In the fitted sample, the residuals sum to zero and their sample inner product with each column of \(X\) is zero. That is an algebraic property of OLS on the observations used in the fit; it doesn’t promise the same relationship on new dates.

The residual sum of squares measures variation around the fitted line:

\[ RSS=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat\varepsilon_t^2, \qquad \hat\sigma^2=\frac{RSS}{T-K-1} \]

The denominator subtracts the \(K\) slopes and the intercept estimated from the same \(T\) observations. Under the classical assumptions of a correctly specified conditional mean, constant error variance, and uncorrelated errors, \(\hat\sigma^2\) estimates the error variance when there are enough observations and the design matrix has full column rank.

Under those assumptions, the estimated covariance matrix of the fitted coefficients is:

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\mid X) =\hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1} \]

The square root of a diagonal entry is the standard error of that coefficient. The off-diagonal entries describe how pairs of coefficient estimates vary together across samples with the same design matrix; they also matter when we calculate uncertainty for a combination of coefficients. Financial time series can have changing volatility and serial dependence, so inference may require standard errors suited to those features.

These calculations describe uncertainty in the fitted coefficients and give us the inputs for the tests and intervals below. We also measure fit with \(R^2\) and explain its relationship to residual variance when we reach the hedge diagnostics.

Coefficient tests and confidence intervals

A coefficient estimate comes with sampling uncertainty. For a fitted coefficient \(\hat\theta_j\), its standard error \(SE(\hat\theta_j)\) is the square root of the corresponding diagonal entry of the estimated covariance matrix above. To test a proposed value \(\theta_{j,0}\), we form:

\[ t_j=\frac{\hat\theta_j-\theta_{j,0}}{SE(\hat\theta_j)} \]

The numerator is the distance between our estimate and the value being tested; the denominator expresses that distance in estimated standard-error units. A common test uses \(\theta_{j,0}=0\) to ask whether the coefficient is distinguishable from zero under the assumed regression model. The associated \(p\)-value measures how unusual a statistic at least as extreme as the observed one would be if the null value and the test assumptions held.

For a two-sided confidence interval, we use a critical value from the relevant reference distribution:

\[ \hat\theta_j\ \pm\ t_{1-\alpha_{sig}/2,\,T-K-1}\,SE(\hat\theta_j) \]

Here \(\alpha_{sig}\) is the chosen significance level and \(T-K-1\) is the residual degrees of freedom. For example, \(\alpha_{sig}=0.05\) gives a 95% interval. The interval describes the range of coefficient values compatible with the estimate at that confidence level under the assumptions used for the standard error. Its width increases when the coefficient is estimated less precisely.

We can also test several restrictions together. If a restricted regression imposes \(q\) restrictions and an unrestricted regression estimates all \(K\) slopes, their residual sums of squares give the classical statistic:

\[ F=\frac{(RSS_R-RSS_U)/q}{RSS_U/(T-K-1)} \]

The numerator measures the fit lost per restriction, and the denominator estimates the unrestricted error variance. Under the classical normal-error assumptions, we compare this statistic with an \(F\) distribution with \(q\) and \(T-K-1\) degrees of freedom. For example, we could test whether a group of hedge coefficients is jointly zero.

We use OLS to estimate hedge ratios, then measure the risk of the resulting positions. In our model tables, we focus on the hedge-return paths and their risk measures; the coefficient tests and confidence intervals above give us additional statistical background for interpreting the fitted coefficients.

From a regression coefficient to a hedge position

For the one-variable regression, the estimated slope is the sample covariance of target and hedge returns divided by the sample variance of the hedge. That is exactly the ratio we need when choosing the hedge that minimizes the variance of a target-plus-hedge book. We’ll derive the minimum-variance result below and then extend the book to several hedge assets.

The intercept has a different role from a hedge coefficient. It locates the regression line, but there is no asset position corresponding to the intercept. We therefore estimate it as part of the regression and use the slopes to size the hedge positions. For one target and one hedge, the fitted regression residual subtracts both the estimated intercept and the hedge contribution; the return of a book holding the target and shorting the hedge subtracts the hedge contribution from the target return.

The observations used to estimate a coefficient also matter. We first fit a regression on historical returns, then apply an available estimate to returns that follow. This turns the fitted relationship into a position whose remaining risk we can measure through time.

1.1 Hedge ratio as a minimum-variance problem

The regression coefficient has a trading meaning. In the one-hedge case, we can derive the optimal hedge ratio by minimizing the variance of the residual book:

\[ \min_{\beta} \; \text{Var}(y_t - \beta x_t) \]

If \(\sigma_y^2\) and \(\sigma_x^2\) are the two return variances and \(\sigma_{xy}\) is their covariance, we can expand the variance:

\[ \text{Var}(y_t - \beta x_t) = \sigma_y^2 - 2\beta\sigma_{xy} + \beta^2\sigma_x^2 \]

Taking the derivative with respect to \(\beta\) and setting it to zero gives:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta}\left(\sigma_y^2 - 2\beta\sigma_{xy} + \beta^2\sigma_x^2\right)= -2\sigma_{xy}+2\beta\sigma_x^2 = 0 \]

So the minimum-variance hedge ratio is:

\[ \beta^* = \frac{\text{Cov}(y_t,x_t)}{\text{Var}(x_t)} \]

With an intercept, the one-variable OLS slope has this same covariance-over-variance form. Estimating the slope from a sample therefore gives us the hedge ratio that minimizes the variance of that sample’s residual book. The intercept shifts the fitted regression line, while the slope determines the position in the hedge asset.

For multiple hedge assets, the same idea becomes:

\[ \boldsymbol{\beta}^* = \Sigma_{xx}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{xy} \]

where \(\Sigma_{xx}\) is the covariance matrix of hedge returns and \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{xy}\) is the vector of covariances between each hedge and the target. This formula also shows why multicollinearity matters. If hedge assets are highly correlated, \(\Sigma_{xx}\) becomes harder to invert, and the estimated beta vector can become unstable.

1.2 Hedge quality and risk reduction

A hedge ratio is meant to reduce an exposure in the target return. The residual can still have a positive or negative average return, so the average return and the reduction in risk answer different questions. For the hedge-ratio comparisons, we focus on how much unwanted systematic risk remains after the hedge is applied.

Several outcomes would make the hedge useful:

\[ \text{Vol}(r^{hedged}) < \text{Vol}(y) \]

\[ ES_{5\%}(r^{hedged}) \text{ is less negative than } ES_{5\%}(y) \]

\[ |\beta(r^{hedged}, x)| < |\beta(y,x)| \]

Each measure captures a different part of the return distribution. A hedge can reduce ordinary day-to-day volatility while leaving a large loss in the tail, and it can remove exposure to a proxy while another source of risk remains in the book. The path of the hedged return can also have a larger drawdown even when its daily volatility is lower.

We’ll therefore look at volatility, tail risk, drawdown, and remaining beta together. The regression’s \(R^2\) helps us understand the fitted relationship, while the realized hedged return lets us evaluate how that relationship performs as a position.

2.1 Financial relationships in the universe

We choose targets and hedges with a clear financial connection. Their shared exposures give us a reason to estimate a relationship, and their differences help us interpret the return left after the hedge. The same regression can have a very different meaning for two bond funds than it has for a stock and its sector basket.

For example, AGG and BND are both broad US aggregate bond ETFs, so their holdings should give them a close common return component. QQQ and SPY both carry US equity exposure, while QQQ has more growth and technology exposure; their residual can therefore contain a growth tilt. TLT and IEF both hold Treasuries, so their hedge ratio should reflect the difference in duration exposure. HYG and LQD both hold corporate credit, but HYG has greater default and equity-cycle sensitivity.

For individual stocks, we use sector and market ETFs as partial explanations. Hedging NVDA with SMH and QQQ removes part of the semiconductor and Nasdaq growth exposure. The remaining return is closer to NVDA-specific information, although it can still contain risk factors that aren’t represented in the hedge basket.

We can compare the regression’s \(R^2\) with the financial role of each hedge and then examine the exposure that remains in the residual. This connects the fit of each relationship with a concrete interpretation of what the target and its hedge assets hold.

3) Target and hedge relationships

We define each hedge problem as a relationship with one target and one or more hedge assets. In a one-hedge case, the model is:

\[ y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + \varepsilon_t \]

If we have \(K\) hedge assets, each with return \(x_{j,t}\) and coefficient \(\beta_j\), the relationship becomes:

\[ y_t = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{1,t} + \beta_2 x_{2,t} + \cdots + \beta_K x_{K,t} + \varepsilon_t \]

or in vector notation:

\[ y_t = \alpha + \mathbf{x}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\beta} + \varepsilon_t \]

The hedge-book return is then:

\[ r^{hedged}_t = y_t - \sum_{j=1}^{K}\beta_{j,t-1}x_{j,t} \]

The lag on \(\beta_{t-1}\) is critical. It means we trade using yesterday’s available hedge ratio, not today’s hindsight hedge ratio. Each coefficient scales the corresponding hedge return, so the book subtracts the combined exposure of all \(K\) hedges.

The relationships are economically different:

  • QQQ versus SPY measures growth-heavy Nasdaq exposure relative to broad US equity beta.
  • TLT versus IEF measures long-duration exposure relative to intermediate-duration Treasuries.
  • AGG versus BND is almost a replication problem between two broad aggregate bond ETFs.
  • HYG versus LQD/TLT/SPY tries to isolate high-yield credit after investment-grade credit, rates, and equity beta.
  • GDX versus GLD/SPY separates gold miners from gold price and equity market components.
  • Single-stock relationships hedge mega-cap or high-beta stocks with sector ETFs and QQQ, trying to remove market/sector beta and leave idiosyncratic residual behavior.

4) Coverage and first diagnostics

Before estimating hedge ratios, we inspect basic coverage and relationship quality. For each target and hedge proxy, we calculate annualized volatility and their return correlation:

\[ \sigma_y = \sqrt{252}\,\text{std}(y_t) \]

\[ \sigma_x = \sqrt{252}\,\text{std}(x_t) \]

\[ \rho_{yx} = \text{corr}(y_t, x_t) \]

When a relationship has several hedge assets, the diagnostic proxy is their equal-weight average return. For a one-factor regression on that proxy, the coefficient of determination is:

\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_t \hat{\varepsilon}_t^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \bar{y})^2} \]

A high \(R^2\) means the hedge proxy explains a large share of the target’s return variance. A low \(R^2\) means the target has a large residual component, so hedging will be incomplete.

We also compute a rolling beta interquartile range:

\[ \text{IQR}(\beta) = Q_{0.75}(\beta_t) - Q_{0.25}(\beta_t) \]

The interquartile range measures the spread between the upper and lower quartiles of the rolling beta path. A relationship can have high return correlation and still have a wide beta IQR, meaning that its estimated hedge ratio changes substantially through time. Dynamic estimates give us a way to follow those changes.

4.2 Residual variance and \(R^2\) intuition

The diagnostic \(R^2\) is useful because it tells us how much of the target’s return variance can be explained by the hedge proxy. In a one-factor regression with intercept:

\[ R^2 = \rho_{yx}^2 \]

So if QQQ and SPY have correlation around \(0.934\), then \(R^2\) is about:

\[ 0.934^2 \approx 0.872 \]

This agrees with the full-sample estimate: about 87% of QQQ’s daily return variance is explained by SPY in this one-factor regression. The remaining 13% is the residual growth/technology component.

For a hedge book, the residual variance is:

\[ \sigma^2_{res} = \sigma_y^2(1-R^2) \]

in the simple one-factor static case. This explains why high-correlation relationships are easier to hedge. If \(R^2\) is high, the residual variance has to be much lower than target variance. If \(R^2\) is low, even the optimal hedge can only remove part of the target risk.

That’s why GDX is hard. Its relationship with GLD and SPY is real, but not dominant. A large part of GDX movement remains outside those hedges.

5) Hedge-book timing and estimation

With the relationships and their initial diagnostics in place, we can now define the timing of the hedge estimates. Daily observations are available from 2015 to the start of 2026, and the first part of that history is used to fit the models before we evaluate their hedge books.

We use the following settings for the first group of hedge models:

  1. Initial training window: two years of daily observations for the first fitted hedge ratio.
  2. Rolling window: one year of daily returns for each updated rolling estimate.
  3. Rebalancing: hedge positions can be updated on Fridays, using the available estimates.
  4. No-trade band: small changes in an estimated hedge ratio don’t always lead to a new position.
  5. Evaluation: the fitted hedge is compared with the unhedged target over the corresponding book dates.

The initial training window is:

\[ L_{train}=504 \quad \text{trading days} \approx 2 \text{ years} \]

The lookback used by rolling estimates is shorter:

\[ L_{roll}=252 \quad \text{trading days} \approx 1 \text{ year} \]

We rebalance weekly on Fridays. An estimate can change on other days, but the position only has a scheduled opportunity to change at the weekly rebalance. The daily book return is still evaluated on each available trading day.

The no-trade band is:

\[ b = 0.05 \]

If the desired hedge ratio differs from the traded ratio by no more than 5 percentage points, we leave that hedge position as it is. A larger difference updates it to the new desired ratio. We’ll use these windows and the weekly schedule when we build the books below, and then write out the band rule alongside the rolling estimates.

5.1 Hedge-book mechanics and static OLS

Now we build the hedge book. Starting with an estimated beta path, we calculate the book returns, turnover, costs, and NAV that let us evaluate the hedge.

For a target \(y_t\) and hedge vector \(\mathbf{x}_t\), the gross hedged return is:

\[ r^{gross}_t = y_t - \boldsymbol{\beta}_{t-1}^\top \mathbf{x}_t \]

The cost-adjusted return is:

\[ r^{net}_t = r^{gross}_t - c_t \]

where:

\[ c_t = \frac{\text{cost bps}}{10000}\sum_{j=1}^{K}|\beta_{j,t}-\beta_{j,t-1}| \]

An estimator that changes its beta frequently generates more trading in the hedge assets. The cost term charges for those changes, allowing us to compare the gross risk reduction with the net return of the position. For each hedge book, we track:

  • volatility reduction: how much annualized volatility falls relative to the unhedged target,
  • expected shortfall reduction: how much the left-tail loss improves,
  • maximum drawdown difference: whether the hedge reduces peak-to-trough pain,
  • beta reduction: how much residual exposure to the hedge proxy remains,
  • turnover and cost drag: whether the model is implementable.

Static OLS is the first benchmark. We estimate one fixed beta from the first 504 observations:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{OLS} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y \]

Here \(X\) includes an intercept and the hedge returns, and \(y\) contains the target returns in the training window. The fitted coefficient vector includes the intercept as its first entry; we use its remaining entries as the hedge positions. We hold the estimated hedge ratio through the rest of the sample, with weekly book evaluation. Static OLS is simple and low-turnover, but it assumes the relationship is stable after the training period.

Our first hedge estimate uses the initial two-year training window. After that window, the hedge book is evaluated through time using the estimate available for each date; the static estimate stays fixed, while the other estimators can produce an updated path. The return on each date uses the previously available hedge position.

We charge 5 basis points on changes in the hedge positions. For a day’s total absolute change in hedge weights, the resulting trading cost is:

\[ \text{cost}_t = \text{turnover}_t \times \frac{5}{10000} \]

The turnover term sums the absolute changes across the hedge assets, so adding or reducing a position both contribute to the amount traded. We subtract that cost from the gross hedge return, as in the book equation above, and compare the resulting net return and risk measures across the models. This lets us see the effect of updating a hedge more often as well as the risk it removes.

def ols_fit(y, x):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    if x.ndim == 1:
        x = x.reshape(-1, 1)
    y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float).reshape(-1)
    xmat = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y)), x])
    coef = np.linalg.lstsq(xmat, y, rcond=None)[0]
    return float(coef[0]), np.asarray(coef[1:], dtype=float)

def hedge_book(ret, r, beta, cost_bps=cost_bps):
    panel = ret[r.assets].dropna()
    b = beta.reindex(panel.index.union(beta.index)).sort_index().ffill().reindex(panel.index)
    b = b.shift(1).dropna(how="any")
    panel = panel.reindex(b.index).dropna(how="any")
    b = b.reindex(panel.index)
    gross = panel[r.target] - (b[r.hedges] * panel[r.hedges]).sum(axis=1)
    turnover = b.diff().abs().sum(axis=1)
    if len(turnover):
        turnover.iloc[0] = b.iloc[0].abs().sum()
    cost = turnover * cost_bps / 10000.0
    net = gross - cost
    return {"target_return": panel[r.target], "gross_return": gross, "net_return": net,
            "turnover": turnover, "cost": cost, "beta": b,
            "gross_values": (1 + gross).cumprod(), "net_values": (1 + net).cumprod(),
            "target_values": (1 + panel[r.target]).cumprod()}

5.2 Reading the hedge metrics

The risk-reduction metrics have specific meanings. Volatility reduction is:

\[ \text{vol\_red} = 1 - \frac{\sigma_{hedged}}{\sigma_{target}} \]

We compare expected shortfalls in the same way, using absolute loss magnitudes:

\[ \text{es\_red} = 1 - \frac{|ES_{hedged}|}{|ES_{target}|} \]

where \(ES\) is the average loss inside the left tail. If \(ES_{hedged}\) is much smaller in absolute value, the hedge improves tail risk.

The drawdown metric is reported as an improvement in absolute drawdown:

\[ \text{maxdd\_diff} = |MDD_{target}| - |MDD_{hedged}| \]

Both drawdowns are measured on their respective return paths. Positive values mean the hedge reduced maximum drawdown. Negative values mean the hedged book had a worse drawdown than the original target.

Beta reduction is:

\[ \text{beta\_red} = 1 - \frac{|\beta_{hedged}|}{|\beta_{target}|} \]

This asks whether the residual book really removed exposure to the hedge proxy. If beta reduction is high but drawdown improvement is bad, the hedge removed the intended factor but introduced or exposed another source of risk.

6) Rolling OLS hedge ratios

Static OLS assumes one relationship for the whole out-of-sample period. Rolling OLS relaxes that assumption. At each date \(t\), we estimate beta from the previous 252 trading days:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_t = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{s=t-L+1}^{t}\left(y_s - \alpha_t - \mathbf{x}_s^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_t\right)^2 \]

with \(L=252\). Each estimate uses the trailing window ending at \(t\), so the coefficient can adapt as the return relationship changes across regimes.

Rolling OLS is useful when the true relationship drifts. But it has two statistical weaknesses:

  1. Sampling noise. A 252-day window can be noisy, especially with multiple hedges.
  2. Window cliffs. When one old observation drops out and one new observation enters, the beta can jump even if the economic relation hasn’t changed much.

That’s why we apply the no-trade band after rebalancing:

\[ \beta^{trade}_t = \begin{cases} \beta^{trade}_{t-1}, & |\beta^{desired}_t - \beta^{trade}_{t-1}| \le b \\ \beta^{desired}_t, & |\beta^{desired}_t - \beta^{trade}_{t-1}| > b \end{cases} \]

We apply the rule to each hedge coefficient, turning the noisy desired beta into the beta path we actually trade.

7) Ridge regression for hedge ratios

Next we improve the hedge ratios using ridge regression. It doesn’t make beta time-varying by itself; we still estimate it on rolling windows here. The difference is that ridge penalizes large and unstable coefficients.

The ridge objective is:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{ridge,t} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left[\sum_{s=t-L+1}^{t}\left(y_s - \alpha_t - \mathbf{x}_s^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^2 + \alpha \sum_{j=1}^{K}\beta_j^2 \right] \]

We center and scale the target and hedge returns before applying the penalty, so the coefficient sizes are comparable. We handle the intercept by centering, so it isn’t penalized. For standardized data, the closed-form solution is:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{ridge} = (X_z^\top X_z + \alpha I)^{-1}X_z^\top y_z \]

Then we convert the standardized coefficients back to return units:

\[ \hat{\beta}_{j} = \hat{\beta}^{(z)}_{j}\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_{x_j}} \]

Ridge is especially useful in multi-hedge relationships where hedge assets are correlated with each other. For example, SMH and QQQ both contain growth/technology exposure. XLY and QQQ both contain mega-cap growth components. LQD, TLT, and SPY can all explain parts of HYG during different regimes. In those settings, pure OLS can assign extreme coefficients because it tries to separate correlated explanatory variables too aggressively.

Ridge shrinks the solution toward smaller coefficients. It accepts a little bias in exchange for lower variance and better stability.

alpha = 10.0

def ridge_beta(ret, r):
    z = ret[r.assets].dropna()
    out = pd.DataFrame(np.nan, index=ret.index, columns=r.hedges)

        x = sample[r.hedges].to_numpy(float)
        y = sample[r.target].to_numpy(float)
        xm = x.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)
        ym = y.mean()
        xs = x.std(axis=0, ddof=1)

        out.loc[z.index[i], r.hedges] = b_std * ys / xs
    return out

7.1 Bias-variance tradeoff in hedge estimation

The difference between rolling OLS and ridge is a classic bias-variance tradeoff.

Rolling OLS is unbiased under the usual regression assumptions, but it can have high variance when hedge variables overlap. Ridge adds shrinkage, so the coefficients are biased toward zero, but the estimator becomes more stable:

\[ \text{MSE}(\hat{\beta}) = \text{Bias}(\hat{\beta})^2 + \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}) \]

A hedge ratio is a trading input, so lower estimator variance can be more valuable than zero bias. A slightly biased hedge that trades smoothly can be better than a theoretically unbiased hedge that jumps around and creates cost.

This is especially true in multi-hedge single-stock models. If SMH and QQQ both explain NVDA, OLS may fight over which hedge gets credit. Ridge softens that fight. It doesn’t remove the economic overlap, but it prevents the regression from translating that overlap into extreme coefficient changes.

8) Kalman filter hedge ratios

We now move to the Kalman filter to estimate a hedge ratio that changes as new return observations arrive. With static OLS we use one hedge ratio, and with rolling OLS we re-estimate it on a fixed window. Here we treat the hedge ratio as a hidden state that moves through time and can only be inferred indirectly from the observed returns. We also keep track of the uncertainty around that state estimate as new returns arrive.

The changing beta between two assets isn’t directly visible. We observe daily target and hedge returns, then infer the coefficient that relates them. A surprise in the target return can come from a change in the relationship or from a temporary shock, so we need to account for uncertainty when deciding how much to update beta. A slowly changing relationship should produce a gradual adjustment, while a noisy daily move shouldn’t force a large change by itself.

We can use the Kalman filter to handle this problem in a sequence of steps:

  • it keeps a current belief about the hedge ratio,
  • it predicts what today’s target return should be given that belief,
  • it measures the surprise between the predicted and realized target return,
  • it updates the hedge ratio only as much as the surprise deserves.

This makes it more structured than rolling OLS. Rolling OLS has a hard memory cutoff. The Kalman filter has a soft memory: old information fades because uncertainty accumulates, but it isn’t suddenly dropped from the model.

8.1 The state-space model

To estimate the changing hedge, we write the problem as a state-space model with two equations.

The first equation connects the hidden hedge ratio to the observed target return. This is the observation equation:

\[ y_t = \alpha_t + \mathbf{x}_t^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_t + \varepsilon_t \]

where:

  • \(y_t\) is the target return, for example NVDA’s daily return,
  • \(\mathbf{x}_t\) is the vector of hedge returns, for example SMH and QQQ returns,
  • \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_t\) is the vector of hedge ratios at time \(t\),
  • \(\alpha_t\) is the time-varying intercept,
  • \(\varepsilon_t\) is the return shock the hedge basket didn’t explain.

We assume the unexplained return shock is Gaussian noise:

\[ \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R) \]

The scalar \(R\) is observation noise. If \(R\) is large, daily returns are very noisy relative to the regression relationship. In that case, one bad prediction doesn’t prove beta changed. If \(R\) is small, a forecast error is more informative, so beta can update more.

The second equation describes how the hidden state moves. We collect the intercept and all hedge ratios into one vector:

\[ \boldsymbol{\theta}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_t & \beta_{1,t} & \cdots & \beta_{K,t} \end{bmatrix}^\top \]

If this state follows a random walk, its movement is:

\[ \boldsymbol{\theta}_t = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_t \]

with:

\[ \boldsymbol{\eta}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q) \]

We use the matrix \(Q\) for process noise, which controls how much the hedge ratios are allowed to move from one day to the next. High \(Q\) means we believe the relationship is unstable and beta can move quickly. Low \(Q\) means we believe the relationship is stable and beta should move slowly.

\(R\) is noise in returns. \(Q\) is noise in the hidden beta path. The whole Kalman filter is basically a disciplined balance between these two uncertainties.

8.2 Prediction step: belief before seeing today’s return

At the end of yesterday, we have the filter’s state estimate and its uncertainty:

\[ \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1|t-1}, \qquad P_{t-1|t-1} \]

The notation \(t-1|t-1\) means “our estimate at time \(t-1\) after using information available through time \(t-1\).”

  • \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1|t-1}\) is yesterday’s best estimate of the state, meaning yesterday’s intercept and hedge ratios.
  • \(P_{t-1|t-1}\) is the uncertainty around that estimate. It’s a covariance matrix, so the diagonal entries measure uncertainty around each state component, and the off-diagonal entries measure how those uncertainties move together.

Before seeing today’s return, we predict today’s state. Since the state follows a random walk, the best prediction for today’s beta is yesterday’s beta:

\[ \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t|t-1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1|t-1} \]

The notation \(t|t-1\) means “our estimate for time \(t\) before seeing time \(t\) data.” Under the random-walk assumption, the expected change in the state is zero before the new observation arrives. The actual hidden beta can still move, and the uncertainty matrix accounts for that possible movement.

But our uncertainty does increase:

\[ P_{t|t-1} = P_{t-1|t-1} + Q \]

Our predicted beta remains at yesterday’s estimate, while its uncertainty increases because another day has passed and the hidden relationship could have drifted. The process-noise matrix \(Q\) adds precisely that extra uncertainty to the prediction covariance.

For TLT and IEF, we can carry yesterday’s estimate forward as the starting point for today’s duration hedge. Rates, duration risk, and bond volatility can still change between observations, so the added process uncertainty allows the next return observation to revise that estimate.

8.3 Measurement step: prediction error and its uncertainty

Now we observe today’s hedge returns and target return. To write the regression compactly, we create the design vector:

\[ \mathbf{h}_t = \begin{bmatrix}1 & x_{1,t} & \cdots & x_{K,t}\end{bmatrix}^\top \]

The first element is \(1\) because the state includes an intercept. The remaining elements are today’s hedge returns.

Using the predicted state and today’s observed hedge returns, we can estimate today’s target return:

\[ \hat{y}_{t|t-1} = \mathbf{h}_t^\top\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t|t-1} \]

This is simply the regression prediction using the current beta belief. If the target is HYG and the hedges are LQD, TLT, and SPY, this predicted return is the return HYG “should” have earned based on today’s credit, rate, and equity moves.

Then we compare prediction with reality:

\[ v_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1} \]

This \(v_t\) is called the innovation. It’s the new information in today’s observation. If \(v_t\) is near zero, the old beta explained today’s return well. If \(v_t\) is large, something was missed: either beta changed, the target had an idiosyncratic shock, or the observation is noisy.

To judge how surprising that innovation is, we also need its variance. This is the innovation variance:

\[ S_t = \mathbf{h}_t^\top P_{t|t-1}\mathbf{h}_t + R \]

This equation has two parts:

  • \(\mathbf{h}_t^\top P_{t|t-1}\mathbf{h}_t\) is uncertainty coming from our uncertain state estimate. If beta is poorly known, the prediction is uncertain.
  • \(R\) is observation noise. Even with a perfect beta estimate, daily returns still contain noise.

So \(S_t\) answers: how much prediction error should we expect before calling today’s move a real beta signal? A large \(S_t\) means we expected noisy prediction errors, so a given \(v_t\) isn’t too informative. A small \(S_t\) means the prediction should have been accurate, so the same \(v_t\) matters more.

8.4 Kalman gain and state update

We use the Kalman gain to determine how much the hedge ratio should move after seeing the innovation:

\[ K_t = \frac{P_{t|t-1}\mathbf{h}_t}{S_t} \]

Here \(K_t\) is a vector with one entry for each state component: one for the intercept and one for each hedge ratio. It works like an adaptive learning rate.

The numerator \(P_{t|t-1}\mathbf{h}_t\) says how much uncertainty in each state component matters for today’s prediction. If the SPY hedge coefficient is very uncertain and today’s SPY return is large, the SPY beta component receives more attention. If a hedge return is tiny today, there isn’t much information about that hedge’s beta, even if the prediction error is large.

The denominator \(S_t\) scales the update by total prediction uncertainty. If today’s prediction was expected to be noisy, we update the state less. If today’s prediction was supposed to be precise, we update the state more.

The state update is:

\[ \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t|t} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t|t-1} + K_t v_t \]

Written in words, the state update combines our previous estimate with the new information in today’s return:

\[ \text{new beta belief} = \text{old beta belief} + \text{learning rate} \times \text{forecast surprise} \]

If the target return is higher than predicted, \(v_t > 0\). Depending on the signs and magnitudes inside \(K_t\), the update may raise one or more of the hedge ratios. If the target return is lower than predicted, \(v_t < 0\), the update goes in the opposite direction.

After updating the state, we also update its uncertainty:

\[ P_{t|t} = (I - K_t\mathbf{h}_t^\top)P_{t|t-1} \]

The identity matrix \(I\) keeps dimensions correct. The term \(K_t\mathbf{h}_t^\top\) represents how much information today’s observation gave us about the state. After using a useful observation, we’re more confident than before, so uncertainty falls.

The full cycle is therefore:

  1. carry yesterday’s hedge ratio forward,
  2. add uncertainty because the true beta may have moved,
  3. predict today’s target return,
  4. measure the forecast surprise,
  5. decide how informative the surprise is,
  6. update the hedge ratio,
  7. reduce uncertainty after learning from the observation.

The updated state carries information from earlier observations into the next date. Each new observation is weighted by its innovation and uncertainty, so the hedge path can adjust without assigning the same response to every daily return surprise.

8.5 Likelihood selection for \(Q\) and \(R\)

We need values for \(Q\) and \(R\), and choosing them casually would make the result arbitrary. So we select them from the training window using likelihood.

The likelihood measures how plausible the observed forecast errors are under a candidate pair of \(Q\) and \(R\). For each observation, the Gaussian log-likelihood contribution is:

\[ \ell_t = -\frac{1}{2}\left[\log(2\pi S_t) + \frac{v_t^2}{S_t}\right] \]

where:

  • \(v_t\) is the innovation, the one-step-ahead forecast error,
  • \(S_t\) is the innovation variance,
  • \(\log(2\pi S_t)\) penalizes models that claim too much uncertainty,
  • \(v_t^2/S_t\) penalizes models that produce forecast errors too large relative to their claimed uncertainty.

The total likelihood over the training sample is:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_t \]

We try several process-noise and observation-noise settings, then choose the combination with the best penalized likelihood on the training window. The penalty matters because a very large \(Q\) can make beta chase the target too closely. That may improve in-sample forecast errors, but it creates unstable hedge ratios and high turnover.

We want the chosen \(Q\) and \(R\) to let the hedge follow genuine changes in the relationship without treating every noisy daily return as a change in beta. The training-window likelihood gives us a way to select that balance.

8.6 Kalman filter versus rolling windows

The two estimators use past observations differently when they update the hedge ratio.

Rolling OLS has a hard memory cutoff:

\[ \mathcal{W}_t = \{t-251,\ldots,t\} \]

Everything inside the window is used equally, and everything outside the window is ignored completely. This makes the estimator easy to understand, but it creates artificial jumps when important observations enter or leave the window.

The Kalman filter has soft memory. Past data affects the current state through the previous posterior estimate and its uncertainty. If we’re confident in the current state, one new observation doesn’t move beta much. If our estimate is uncertain, the same observation can move beta more.

Rolling OLS refits on a fixed set of recent observations, while the Kalman estimate carries the previous state forward and updates it using the new observation. The resulting Kalman path can be smoother and trade less, although the response also depends on the chosen process and observation noise. Rolling OLS may react faster when the underlying relationship changes abruptly.

def kf_run(y, x, state0, pcov0, qdiag, obs_var):
    state = state0.copy()
    pcov = pcov0.copy()
    qcov = np.diag(qdiag)
    states, loglik = [], 0.0
    for yy, xx in zip(y, x):
        h = np.r_[1.0, xx]
        pcov = pcov + qcov
        pred = float(h @ state)
        s = float(h @ pcov @ h.T + obs_var)
        err = float(yy - pred)
        loglik += -0.5 * (np.log(2 * np.pi * s) + err * err / s)
        k = (pcov @ h.T) / s
        state = state + k * err
        pcov = (np.eye(len(state)) - np.outer(k, h)) @ pcov
        states.append(state.copy())
    return np.asarray(states), loglik

    resid_var = max(np.nanvar(resid, ddof=1), 1e-8)

    scale = np.r_[0.05, np.maximum(np.abs(b0), 0.25)]
    best_ll, best_q, best_r = -np.inf, np.r_[1e-7, np.repeat(1e-5, len(b0))], resid_var
    for q0 in [1e-7, 3e-7, 1e-6, 3e-6, 1e-5, 3e-5, 1e-4]:
        qdiag = np.maximum(q0 * scale**2, 1e-12)
        for r0 in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0]:
            obs_var = max(resid_var * r0, 1e-10)
            _, ll = kf_run(y_train, x_train, state0, pcov0, qdiag, obs_var)
            ll -= 0.5 * qdiag[1:].sum() / obs_var
            if ll > best_ll:
                best_ll, best_q, best_r = ll, qdiag, obs_var

    y_all = z[r.target].to_numpy(float)
    x_all = z[r.hedges].to_numpy(float)
    states, _ = kf_run(y_all, x_all, state0, pcov0, best_q, best_r)
    out.loc[z.index, r.hedges] = states[:, 1:]
    out.loc[z.index[:n_train - 1], r.hedges] = np.nan
    return out

9) No-trade band sensitivity

After estimating a desired beta path, we decide how aggressively to trade it. We use the no-trade band to control these updates. If the band is zero, every weekly beta change is traded. If the band is wider, small beta changes are ignored.

We test:

\[ b \in \{0.00, 0.025, 0.05, 0.075, 0.10\} \]

For each band, we recompute the hedge books for rolling, ridge, and Kalman models, then evaluate average risk reduction, turnover, cost, and a composite score.

The composite score is intentionally multi-objective. It rewards:

  • higher volatility reduction,
  • higher ES reduction,
  • better drawdown improvement,
  • stronger beta reduction,
  • more stable beta paths,

and penalizes:

  • turnover,
  • cost drag,
  • negative drawdown effects.

A pure volatility score would be too narrow. A hedge model can reduce variance by taking an unstable residual exposure that creates drawdown or trading costs. Including drawdown and trading costs lets us compare those risks along with the reduction in variance.

def normalize_score(s, lower=False):
    x = pd.to_numeric(s, errors="coerce").replace([np.inf, -np.inf], np.nan)
    if x.notna().sum() <= 1:
        out = pd.Series(0.5, index=s.index)
    else:
        lo, hi = x.quantile(0.05), x.quantile(0.95)
        xc = x.clip(lo, hi)
        span = xc.max() - xc.min()
        out = pd.Series(0.5, index=s.index) if span <= 1e-12 else (xc - xc.min()) / span
    return (1 - out if lower else out).fillna(0.5)


def score_table(tab):
    parts = []
    for _, g in tab.groupby("relationship", sort=False):
        h = g.copy()
        vol_s = normalize_score(h["vol_red"])
        dd_bad = ((-pd.to_numeric(h["maxdd_diff"], errors="coerce"))
                  .clip(lower=0.0).div(0.10).clip(upper=1.0).fillna(0.0))
        vol_s = vol_s.mask(h["maxdd_diff"] < -0.05, np.minimum(vol_s, 0.50))
        h["score"] = (0.30 * vol_s + 0.20 * normalize_score(h["es_red"])
                      + 0.15 * normalize_score(h["maxdd_diff"]) + 0.15 * normalize_score(h["beta_red"])
                      + 0.10 * normalize_score(h["beta_stability"])
                      - 0.05 * (1 - normalize_score(h["turnover_ann"], lower=True))
                      - 0.05 * (1 - normalize_score(h["cost_drag_ann"], lower=True)) - 0.10 * dd_bad)
        parts.append(h)
    return pd.concat(parts, ignore_index=True) if parts else pd.DataFrame()

10) Model scoring and best hedge selection

Now we compare the static, rolling, ridge, and Kalman results. We score the models within each relationship, so the same metric is compared among models applied to the same target and hedge basket.

A 40% volatility reduction in TSLA isn’t the same difficulty as a 40% reduction in AGG. AGG/BND is a clean ETF replication problem. TSLA/XLY+QQQ is a much harder idiosyncratic stock problem. Scoring within each relationship avoids unfairly rewarding easy pairs only because they are easy.

For relationship \(i\) and model \(m\), we normalize the components within the relationship and calculate the score using:

\[ \text{score}_{i,m} = 0.30S_{vol} + 0.20S_{ES} + 0.15S_{DD} + 0.15S_{\beta} + 0.10S_{stable} - 0.05P_{turnover} - 0.05P_{cost} - 0.10P_{badDD} \]

These weights define the ranking used in our comparison: we give most weight to volatility and tail-risk reduction while also accounting for drawdown, beta stability, and trading. Our aim is risk reduction with stable, tradable hedge ratios.

The two most important additional diagnostics are:

\[ \text{beta stability} = \frac{1}{1 + \text{IQR}(\beta) + 5\cdot \text{mean}|\Delta \beta|} \]

and:

\[ \text{score gap} = \text{best score} - \text{second best score} \]

A large score gap means one model clearly dominates for that relationship. A small score gap means the choice is less certain.

10.1 Model choice across hedge relationships

There’s no reason to expect one estimator to dominate every hedge problem. The underlying economics decide what kind of estimator is appropriate.

For replication relationships, like AGG/BND, the true beta is stable. Static OLS can win because dynamic models only add noise.

For factor-overlap relationships, like NVDA/SMH+QQQ, ridge can work well because the hedge assets explain common factors and can be correlated with each other. It can also shrink a single coefficient, as in QQQ/SPY.

For regime-sensitive relationships, like HYG credit or GDX gold miners, Kalman can help because exposures change across stress, recovery, inflation, and liquidity regimes.

For fast-changing relationships, rolling OLS can win because it responds faster than a conservative Kalman filter. That responsiveness costs more turnover, but sometimes the risk reduction is worth it.

Scoring each relationship separately lets us compare estimators applied to the same target and hedge basket. We can then connect the selected model to the relationship’s stability, overlap between hedge assets, and the amount of change in its exposures.

11) Residual spreads and stationarity testing

In the first part, we used hedge ratios for risk reduction. Now we’ll use price hedge ratios for residual trading.

The return hedge above reduces exposure to a set of assets. To trade a residual spread, we also want its deviations to return toward an equilibrium. A spread with low volatility can still drift for years, so we need to examine its behavior through time before using a deviation as a trading signal.

For residual trading we switch from returns to log prices. For a target price \(P^y_t\) and hedge price \(P^x_t\), the price relationship is:

\[ \log P^y_t = \alpha_t + \beta_t \log P^x_t + s_t \]

where:

  • \(\log P^y_t\) is the target log price,
  • \(\log P^x_t\) is the hedge log price,
  • \(\beta_t\) is the price hedge ratio or cointegrating slope,
  • \(\alpha_t\) is the intercept,
  • \(s_t\) is the residual spread.

Rearranging gives the spread directly:

\[ s_t = \log P^y_t - \alpha_t - \beta_t\log P^x_t \]

If \(s_t\) is stationary, deviations from its mean are temporary. A high \(s_t\) means the target is expensive relative to the hedge after adjusting for the estimated long-run relationship. A low \(s_t\) means the target is cheap relative to the hedge. This creates the logic for a mean-reversion trade.

If \(s_t\) has a unit root, the situation is different. A high spread can stay high, move higher, or reflect a permanent economic break. In that case, z-score trading becomes dangerous because we keep expecting a reversal that may never arrive, even after the spread moves a long way from its recent mean.

We’ll use stationarity and related tests to assess each price spread before using its deviations as trading signals. The tests and the trading rules give us two ways to study the same relative-price relationship.

11.1 ADF, Engle-Granger, and half-life

The Augmented Dickey-Fuller test checks whether the spread behaves like a stationary process or like a drifting process with a unit root. A simple version of the test regression is:

\[ \Delta s_t = a + \gamma s_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\Delta s_{t-j} + u_t \]

Here:

  • \(\Delta s_t = s_t - s_{t-1}\) is the daily change in the spread,
  • \(s_{t-1}\) is yesterday’s spread level,
  • \(a\) is a constant drift term,
  • the lagged differences \(\Delta s_{t-j}\) control for autocorrelation in spread changes,
  • \(u_t\) is the remaining error term,
  • \(\gamma\) measures the pull of the previous spread level on the current change.

The hypotheses are:

\[ H_0: \gamma = 0 \quad \text{unit root, non-stationary spread} \]

\[ H_1: \gamma < 0 \quad \text{stationary, mean-reverting spread} \]

The sign of \(\gamma\) matters. If \(s_{t-1}\) is high relative to its equilibrium and \(\gamma < 0\), the expected change \(\Delta s_t\) is negative, pulling the spread back down. If \(s_{t-1}\) is below that equilibrium, the same negative \(\gamma\) implies a positive expected move. This is mean reversion in regression form.

Engle-Granger cointegration is related, but the starting question is slightly different. It asks whether two non-stationary price series share a stable long-run relationship. First we estimate:

\[ y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + s_t \]

Then we test whether \(s_t\) is stationary. If \(y_t\) and \(x_t\) individually wander through time but their residual \(s_t\) mean-reverts, the pair is cointegrated. Financially, this means the two assets can drift, trend, and compound, but their relative valuation has an equilibrium pull.

Half-life translates the strength of mean reversion into trading language. We estimate:

\[ \Delta s_t = a + b s_{t-1} + e_t \]

and approximate:

\[ \text{half-life} = -\frac{\ln 2}{b}, \quad b < 0 \]

This expression is an approximation that works best when the estimated reversion coefficient is small in magnitude. If the half-life is 10 trading days, a spread shock is expected to decay by about half in roughly two weeks. If the half-life is 60 trading days, the same trade may require three months of patience. If the half-life is infinite or negative in the wrong way, the spread isn’t mean-reverting enough for this setup.

Here we require a half-life between 2 and 63 trading days. Very fast spreads are hard to trade after costs. Very slow spreads can trap capital and generate painful drawdowns before the expected reversion happens.

def kf_price_beta(px, target, hedge):
    lp = np.log(px[[target, hedge]][px[[target, hedge]] > 0]).dropna()
    out = pd.DataFrame(np.nan, index=px.index, columns=["alpha", "beta"])
    a, b = ols_fit(lp[target].iloc[:n_train], lp[[hedge]].iloc[:n_train])
    state = np.array([a, b[0]], dtype=float)
    h0 = np.column_stack([np.ones(n_train), lp[hedge].iloc[:n_train].to_numpy(float)])
    resid = lp[target].iloc[:n_train].to_numpy(float) - h0 @ state
    obs_var = max(np.nanvar(resid, ddof=1), 1e-8)
    pcov = np.eye(2) * 0.10
    qcov = np.diag([1e-7, 1e-5])
    out.loc[lp.index[n_train - 1], ["alpha", "beta"]] = state
    for i in range(n_train, len(lp)):
        h = np.array([1.0, lp[hedge].iloc[i]])
        pcov = pcov + qcov
        s = float(h @ pcov @ h.T + obs_var)
        k = (pcov @ h.T) / s
        state = state + k * (lp[target].iloc[i] - h @ state)
        pcov = (np.eye(2) - np.outer(k, h)) @ pcov
        out.loc[lp.index[i], ["alpha", "beta"]] = state
    return out


def log_spread(px, target, hedge, beta):
    lp = np.log(px[[target, hedge]][px[[target, hedge]] > 0])
    b = beta.reindex(lp.index).ffill()
    return (lp[target] - b["alpha"] - b["beta"] * lp[hedge]).replace([np.inf, -np.inf], np.nan)


def eg_test(y, x):
    z = pd.concat([y, x], axis=1).dropna()
    return float(coint(z.iloc[:, 0], z.iloc[:, 1])[1]) if len(z) >= 30 else np.nan


def adf_test(s):
    x = pd.Series(s).dropna()
    try:
        return float(adfuller(x, autolag="AIC")[1]) if len(x) >= 30 else np.nan
    except (ValueError, np.linalg.LinAlgError):
        return np.nan


def half_life(s):
    x = pd.Series(s).dropna()
    z = pd.concat([x.diff(), x.shift(1)], axis=1).dropna()
    if len(z) < 30:
        return np.nan
    _, b = ols_fit(z.iloc[:, 0], z.iloc[:, [1]])
    return -np.log(2) / b[0] if b[0] < 0 else np.inf

11.2 Z-score trading rule

After a spread passes the statistical gate, we need a way to turn its deviations into entry and exit decisions. We use a rolling z-score:

\[ z_t = \frac{s_t - \bar{s}_{t,126}}{\sigma_{s,t,126}} \]

where:

  • \(s_t\) is today’s residual spread,
  • \(\bar{s}_{t,126}\) is the 126-day rolling mean of the spread,
  • \(\sigma_{s,t,126}\) is the 126-day rolling standard deviation,
  • \(z_t\) measures how many recent standard deviations the spread is away from its local mean.

The rule is simple:

Condition Position logic
\(z_t > 2\) target is rich relative to hedge, short the spread
\(z_t < -2\) target is cheap relative to hedge, long the spread
\(|z_t| < 0.5\) spread has mostly normalized, exit
\(|z_t| > 3.5\) move is too extreme, stop and cool down

A very large z-score can reflect a substantial move away from the recent relationship, so we close the position at the stop threshold. The five-trading-day cool-down period then keeps us from immediately re-entering after that large adverse move.

We lag the signal by one day before trading, so we don’t use same-day information incorrectly. If the spread position is \(q_t \in \{-1,0,+1\}\), the daily strategy return is:

\[ r^{strat}_t = q_{t-1}\left(r^y_t - \beta_{t-1}r^x_t\right) - \text{cost}_t \]

The term inside parentheses is the hedged residual return. The lagged \(q_{t-1}\) means the position was decided before today’s return was known. The cost term subtracts trading costs from position changes.

The resulting position trades mean reversion in the residual. Its payoff depends on the target’s movement relative to the hedge and on the estimated relationship returning toward its recent equilibrium.

def z_signal(spread):
    z = (spread - spread.rolling(z_win).mean()) / spread.rolling(z_win).std()
    position, cooldown, positions = 0.0, 0, []
    for value in z:
        if np.isfinite(value):
            if abs(value) > z_stop:
                position = 0.0
                cooldown = z_cool
            elif cooldown > 0:
                position = 0.0
                cooldown -= 1
            elif position == 0 and value > z_in:
                position = -1.0
            elif position == 0 and value < -z_in:
                position = 1.0
            elif abs(value) < z_out:
                position = 0.0
        positions.append(position)
    return pd.DataFrame({"z": z, "signal": pd.Series(positions, index=z.index).shift(1).fillna(0.0)})


def spread_book(ret, target, hedge, beta, signal, cost_bps=cost_bps):
    panel = ret[[target, hedge]].dropna()
    b = beta["beta"].reindex(panel.index).ffill().shift(1)
    sig = signal.reindex(panel.index).fillna(0.0)
    data = pd.concat([panel, b.rename("beta"), sig.rename("signal")], axis=1).dropna()
    spread_ret = data[target] - data["beta"] * data[hedge]
    gross = data["signal"] * spread_ret
    weights = pd.DataFrame({target: data["signal"], hedge: -data["signal"] * data["beta"]})
    turnover = weights.diff().abs().sum(axis=1)
    if len(turnover):
        turnover.iloc[0] = weights.iloc[0].abs().sum()
    cost = turnover * cost_bps / 10000.0
    net = gross - cost
    return {"target_return": spread_ret, "gross_return": gross, "net_return": net,
            "turnover": turnover, "cost": cost,
            "beta": beta[["beta"]].rename(columns={"beta": hedge}).reindex(data.index).ffill(),
            "gross_values": (1 + gross).cumprod(), "net_values": (1 + net).cumprod(),
            "target_values": (1 + spread_ret).cumprod()}

11.3 Dynamic residuals and the trading gate

With dynamic hedge ratios, we make the spreads more stationary, but we have to interpret that result carefully. If \(\beta_t\) changes through time, the spread is no longer the residual of one fixed long-run relationship. It’s the residual after allowing the relationship itself to move.

That can be useful for trading because markets aren’t static. Currency regimes, duration relationships, and sector betas change. But it also means an ADF test on a dynamic residual isn’t the same as proving classical cointegration under a fixed beta.

For that reason, we use different gate conditions for static and dynamic sources. Static spreads are expected to pass a stricter long-run relation test. Dynamic spreads are allowed if the resulting residual is stationary, tradable, and not created by excessive beta turnover.

We also impose a limit on the average absolute change in the price hedge ratio:

\[ \overline{|\Delta \beta|} \le 0.25 \]

Without this filter, a hyperactive beta could force almost any spread to look stationary by chasing the target too closely. We want adaptive residuals, not overfit residuals.

14) International ETF transfer with the library

Now we repeat the same workflow on international ETFs using the quantfinlab hedging tools. These ETFs bring in currency and country exposures alongside the market and sector risks we considered in the main examples. We can compare how those different exposures behave across country and currency ETFs.

For reproduction, check the international_hedging_etfs folder in the data directory. We read the generated international ETF panel locally; the folder explains how to create it. We also use the supporting ETF sources from the core cross-asset and sector ETF data folders for the hedge assets.

International equity ETFs combine several overlapping risk layers:

  • local equity market beta, such as Japan, Europe, China, Brazil, India, Taiwan, or Korea,
  • currency translation, because unhedged country ETFs are priced in USD even when the local market moves in another currency,
  • global equity beta, because most country ETFs still sell off when global risk appetite collapses,
  • commodity sensitivity, especially for Canada, Australia, and Brazil,
  • regional supply-chain exposure, especially Taiwan and Korea through semiconductors,
  • dollar liquidity pressure, because a strong US dollar often tightens financial conditions for emerging markets.

We choose hedge baskets to represent these layers in each country’s returns. The fitted coefficients then tell us how the target moves with the available equity, currency, and commodity proxies, while the remaining return captures exposures outside the chosen basket.

14.0 International hedging as economic decomposition

With these country ETFs, we can test whether hedge ratios separate their returns into recognizable macro components and the residual left over.

For US single stocks, the hedge problem is usually sector and market beta. For international ETFs, the target return can be decomposed more like:

\[ r^{country}_t \approx \beta_1 r^{local\ equity}_t + \beta_2 r^{FX}_t + \beta_3 r^{global\ risk}_t + \beta_4 r^{commodity}_t + \varepsilon_t \]

This equation isn’t one universal model for every country. It’s a template for thinking. The relevant components differ by market:

  • Japan needs yen exposure and a currency-hedged Japan ETF.
  • Europe needs euro exposure and a currency-hedged eurozone ETF.
  • Canada and Australia need commodity and local currency exposure.
  • Taiwan and Korea need semiconductor and global technology exposure.
  • Brazil and India need EM beta and dollar-pressure controls.
  • China needs China-specific proxies rather than only broad EM.

A strong result here means the hedge model is capturing real economic structure. If EFA can be hedged by HEFA and UUP, we’re separating developed international equity from currency translation. If Canada can be hedged by SPY, XLE, DBC, and FXC, we’re decomposing Canada into US/global equity linkage, energy/commodity exposure, and Canadian-dollar movement.

Applying the same estimators across these country ETFs lets us compare how much of each target’s movement is captured by its chosen macro proxies. The remaining residual can then be interpreted alongside the country and sector exposures in that basket.

14.2 Currency-hedged ETFs as natural controls

For some of the cleanest international relationships, we use currency-hedged ETFs as controls. HEFA, HEZU, and DXJ aren’t generic hedge assets. They are designed to hold similar local equity exposure while reducing foreign-exchange exposure.

A simplified unhedged USD return can be written as:

\[ r^{USD}_{country} \approx r^{local}_{equity} + r^{FX} \]

A currency-hedged version tries to isolate:

\[ r^{hedged}_{country} \approx r^{local}_{equity} \]

So the difference between unhedged and hedged ETF returns is strongly connected to currency movement:

\[ r^{USD}_{country} - r^{hedged}_{country} \approx r^{FX} \]

For EFA/HEFA and EZU/HEZU, the currency-hedged ETF gives us an economically matched equity exposure, and the currency proxy helps capture the translation component of the unhedged target. The resulting baskets have the ingredients needed to explain much of the target’s return movement over the sample.

14.3 International hedge model results

For several international relationships, we obtain stronger risk reduction than in the main single-stock examples. The strongest cases also have a clear economic interpretation.

EZU/HEZU is the standout. Kalman is selected, with about 91.3% volatility reduction and 91.4% ES reduction. This is an extremely high hedge-quality result. The reason is that unhedged eurozone equity can be decomposed into hedged eurozone equity, euro exposure, and global equity risk. Once HEZU, FXE, and SPY are in the hedge basket, little unexplained broad risk remains.

EFA/HEFA is also very strong, with static selected and about 85.4% volatility reduction and 87.2% ES reduction. Static working here is meaningful. It means the relationship between unhedged developed ex-US equity, currency-hedged developed ex-US equity, and dollar exposure is stable enough that a fixed beta already performs well.

EWJ/DXJ reaches about 79.0% volatility reduction with Kalman selected. Japan has a clean currency-hedged/unhedged structure, and the yen ETF helps absorb the currency component. The Kalman selection suggests the currency and equity-beta relationship isn’t constant through time, which makes sense because yen behavior changes across risk-off, low-rate, and policy-divergence regimes.

FXI macro is also strong, with Kalman selected and around 79.8% volatility reduction. The hedge basket decomposes FXI’s China equity exposure across several related markets and styles. MCHI, KWEB, ASHR, and UUP together explain a large amount of FXI’s movement because they capture broad China, internet/ADR-heavy China exposure, A-share exposure, and dollar pressure.

The weaker relationships are economically interesting:

  • EWZ Brazil has only about 27.7% volatility reduction, even with ridge selected.
  • EWW Mexico has about 28.9% volatility reduction, with Kalman selected.
  • INDA India is around 32.9%, with rolling selected.

These markets contain local risk that broad proxies don’t fully capture. Brazil has commodity exposure, EM beta, currency pressure, local politics, and fiscal risk all mixed together. Mexico has strong US linkage but also local policy and currency behavior. India often has strong domestic growth and flow dynamics that don’t map cleanly to EEM, QQQ, and UUP.

The weaker scores leave us with sizeable residual exposure after the global and regional proxies have been applied. Those returns can still contain country-specific sector weights, currency moves, political risks, and local earnings cycles.

Across the international relationships, we get a different model ranking from the main universe. Rolling has the highest median score, around 0.584, and Kalman is extremely close at 0.578. Kalman wins the most relationships, with 6 wins, while rolling wins 2 and ridge wins 3.

In the main universe, ridge had the highest median score. In the international universe, Kalman becomes more competitive as country exposures and currency components evolve through time. Exchange-rate regimes, commodity cycles, and regional equity betas change, giving a time-varying hedge ratio room to follow those movements.

Looking at the achieved risk reductions, we can group the relationships into a useful economic hierarchy:

  • currency-hedged/unhedged relationships are highly hedgeable: EZU/HEZU, EFA/HEFA, EWJ/DXJ,
  • close China proxy relationships are highly hedgeable: FXI macro,
  • commodity-linked developed markets are moderately hedgeable: Canada and Australia,
  • emerging markets with local/political/currency residuals are harder: Brazil, Mexico, India.

The same hedge comparison gives us a way to study global ETF structure: the amount of remaining risk depends on how closely each basket matches the target’s equity, currency, and commodity exposures.

14.4 Country residuals as economic signals

The lower-quality international hedges leave more risk in the residual. We can interpret that remaining movement by looking at the local exposures that broad equity, commodity, and currency proxies don’t fully capture.

Brazil is a good example. EWZ is linked to EM beta, commodities, rates, the dollar, local politics, fiscal risk, and the Brazilian real. DBC and UUP help, but they don’t fully capture Petrobras, Vale, local policy, and idiosyncratic country cycles. A low hedge score here is economically believable.

Mexico is also distinct. EWW has US supply-chain and nearshoring exposure, but it also has local currency, rates, financials, and political risk. SPY, EEM, and UUP explain part of it but leave meaningful residual structure.

India is another special case. INDA has EM and growth exposure, but India’s domestic earnings cycle, policy environment, and foreign-flow dynamics often behave differently from broad EM. So a moderate hedge result is more realistic than an artificially high one.

The model scores and residual returns help us compare how closely each market can be replicated by its basket. They also give us a starting point for interpreting the local risks that remain after the common proxies have been hedged.

14.5 International residual gates

We apply the international residual gate to five economically related pairs:

\[ \text{EFA/HEFA},\quad \text{EWJ/DXJ},\quad \text{EZU/HEZU},\quad \text{FXI/MCHI},\quad \text{EWT/EWY} \]

Each pair has a financial relationship that gives us a reason to examine a possible long-run equilibrium:

  • EFA and HEFA are unhedged and currency-hedged versions of developed ex-US equity.
  • EWJ and DXJ are unhedged and currency-hedged Japan equity structures.
  • EZU and HEZU are unhedged and currency-hedged eurozone equity structures.
  • FXI and MCHI are close China equity proxies with different index composition.
  • EWT and EWY represent Taiwan and Korea, two semiconductor-heavy Asian equity markets.

Static log-OLS fails for all of them. That means a fixed price spread isn’t stationary enough over the full sample. This is common for international ETFs because currencies, country leadership, and regional growth stories can drift for years.

Rolling log-OLS and Kalman log versions pass the stationarity-style gates in several cases. For example:

  • EFA/HEFA rolling has ADF p-value around 0.0028 and half-life around 54.5 days,
  • EWJ/DXJ rolling has ADF p-value around 0.0025 and half-life around 44.3 days,
  • EZU/HEZU rolling has ADF p-value around 0.0022 and half-life around 52.5 days,
  • FXI/MCHI rolling has ADF p-value around 0.0001 and half-life around 30.3 days,
  • EWT/EWY rolling has ADF p-value around 0.0010 and half-life around 46.6 days.

These half-lives are plausible for swing-style residual trades. But passing the gate still doesn’t guarantee a profitable strategy. The test measures the spread’s mean-reverting structure in the sample, while the result of a trade also depends on the chosen entry and exit levels, transaction costs, and any changes in the relationship during the holding period.

14.6 International residual trading and final plot

The selected international residual strategies produce different return paths. We can compare their net returns, volatility, and drawdowns to see how the same spread-trading rules behaved across the country and currency relationships.

FXI/MCHI rolling log-OLS is the best secondary residual trade. It has about 15.4% net return, annualized volatility around 4.7%, Sharpe around 0.36, and max drawdown around -10.5%. The economic story is reasonable. FXI and MCHI are both China equity exposures, but they differ in holdings, offshore/onshore composition, index construction, and sensitivity to internet/platform names. Those differences can create temporary deviations without becoming totally unrelated assets.

EZU/HEZU is modestly positive, with net return around 6.5% and Sharpe around 0.17. That modest return is consistent with the idea that eurozone unhedged versus hedged exposure plus euro movement can create mean-reverting residuals, but the edge isn’t large after costs.

EFA/HEFA and EWJ/DXJ are roughly flat to slightly negative. Both relationships provide excellent risk reduction in the hedge comparison, while their selected spread trades produce roughly flat to slightly negative results. A close hedge can leave a spread with limited opportunity after costs, or a spread that returns toward its mean too slowly for the trading rule.

EWT/EWY is the clear failure, with net return around -36.3%, Sharpe around -0.38, and max drawdown near -48.6%. Taiwan and Korea share semiconductor and EM exposure, but their residual spread can trend when one market structurally outperforms the other. In the recent AI and semiconductor cycle, Taiwan-related exposure could dominate Korea for reasons a simple mean-reversion rule doesn’t capture.

In the final 3-by-3 comparison, we can bring together the hedge and residual-trading results:

  • high hedge scores appear in currency-hedged and close-proxy relationships,
  • the risk-reduction scatter confirms that most international hedges reduce volatility and ES substantially,
  • the residual NAV panel shows that residual trading is much more selective than hedging,
  • FXI/MCHI works reasonably, EZU/HEZU is mild, and EWT/EWY is a warning that statistical eligibility isn’t enough when the economic spread can trend.
