18. Rough Volatility for Option Pricing

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code

In this project we move from stochastic volatility to rough volatility. The goal is to understand why the volatility process can look much more irregular than a Brownian path, how we can estimate that roughness from realized variance and option skew, and how rough models change pricing, calibration, and hedge sensitivities.

We focus on five main layers:

  • roughness as a statistical property of log realized variance, measured with fractional Brownian motion and moment scaling,
  • rough-kernel forecasting, compared with HAR and GARCH-style variance forecasts,
  • short-maturity skew power laws, where options reveal roughness through the shape of the implied-volatility surface,
  • rough Bergomi and rough Heston, two different ways to put rough volatility into option pricing,
  • model comparison and secondary BTC implementation, where we test whether the same logic survives in a much noisier crypto option market.

We won’t re-explain option payoffs, implied volatility, Greeks, local volatility, Heston, COS pricing, or American-option numerics from scratch. Those are already covered in Project 4, Project 8, Project 11, Project 13, and Project 14. Here the new story is the regularity of volatility paths and what happens when pricing models respect that regularity.

The main datasets are reused. To reproduce them, check the relevant folders in the data reproducibility layer:

  • underlying_yfinance for SPX and other underlying price histories,
  • spx_optionsdx for SPX option chains,
  • us_treasury_yields for the risk-free curve,
  • btc_yfinance for BTC spot history,
  • btc_optionsdx for BTC/Deribit option chains.

The literature behind the project is also important. The rough-volatility idea became central after empirical work showing that log volatility behaves close to fractional Brownian motion with \(H < 1/2\), often much lower than the Brownian value \(H = 1/2\). Option-price evidence then connected this to short-maturity implied-volatility skew. Rough Bergomi and rough Heston are two model families that translate this empirical fact into pricing engines.

1) Setup, Reused Data, and the New Object

The first code cell imports the option, surface, Fourier, volatility-forecasting, and rough-volatility tools. Most of those objects have already appeared in earlier projects. The new modules are the ones around rough realized-variance diagnostics, rough-kernel forecasts, rBergomi simulation, rough-Heston calibration, and fractional Riccati stability.

The SPX side uses three inputs:

  • SPX spot history, used to build realized variance and log-variance proxies,
  • SPX option chains, used to reconstruct implied-volatility surfaces and short-end skew,
  • Treasury yields, used to attach discounting and forward/carry estimates to option prices.

The important new object is not the price \(S_t\) and not even implied volatility \(\sigma_{\text{imp}}(K,T)\) by itself. The new object is the regularity of the volatility path. We want to understand how jagged the process is when we zoom into time.

A useful way to phrase the problem is:

\[ \text{how fast do volatility increments shrink when the time step shrinks?} \]

For a process \(X_t\), the size of a small increment over a time step \(\Delta\) is roughly controlled by a power law:

\[ |X_{t+\Delta} - X_t| \sim C \Delta^H \]

Here \(H\) is the Hurst exponent. If \(H\) is high, increments shrink quickly as \(\Delta\) gets smaller, so paths look smoother. If \(H\) is low, increments shrink slowly, so the path stays jagged even at small time scales.

Brownian motion has \(H = 1/2\). Rough volatility means the volatility-related process has \(H < 1/2\). A value like \(H = 0.1\) means the path is much rougher than Brownian motion. This is already a strong shift from the classical stochastic-volatility models in Project 11, where variance is usually driven by Markovian Brownian shocks with much smoother short-time behavior.

The key financial idea is simple: if volatility is rough, then very short-dated option smiles can move and steepen much faster than classical Markovian diffusion models naturally expect.

1.1 Physical Roughness and Risk-Neutral Roughness

There are two roughness stories running in parallel.

The first is physical roughness, estimated from realized returns. It uses the historical probability measure \(\mathbb{P}\) because it is based on what actually happened:

\[ X_t^{\mathbb{P}} = \log RV_t \]

When we estimate \(H\) from this series, we are asking how the realized variance process behaves in historical data. This connects naturally to forecasting because a forecast is also a physical-measure object:

\[ \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}_t[RV_{t,t+h}] \]

The second is risk-neutral roughness, estimated from option prices. It uses the pricing measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) because option prices are expectations under risk-neutral pricing plus discounting:

\[ C_t(K,T) = D(t,T)\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_t[(S_T-K)^+] \]

When we estimate \(H\) from ATM skew, we are not estimating the same thing as historical log variance. We are estimating the roughness that the option market is pricing into short-maturity smiles:

\[ |\partial_k \sigma_{\text{imp}}(0,\tau)| \sim c\tau^{H-\frac{1}{2}} \]

These two values of \(H\) can differ. A historical \(H\) comes from realized variance. An option-implied \(H\) comes from option prices, risk premia, hedging flows, and market demand for convexity. In SPX, short-dated downside protection is a structural market object. In BTC, short-dated option skew can also reflect leverage and liquidation risk. So we don’t force the two \(H\) estimates to be equal. We compare them and use each one where it belongs.

This separation is important for the implementation:

Object Measure Used for Main diagnostic
Log realized variance \(\mathbb{P}\) roughness estimation and forecasting moment scaling
ATM implied skew \(\mathbb{Q}\) option-implied roughness skew power law
rBergomi \(\mathbb{Q}\) Monte Carlo smile generation IV fit and path behavior
Rough Heston \(\mathbb{Q}\) Fourier-style pricing and calibration price/IV residuals and Riccati convergence

This project is strong because it doesn’t only fit one model. It tests roughness from both realized variance and option prices, then checks whether rough models actually improve pricing and Greeks.

2) Realized Variance and Log-Variance Proxies

We now build realized-variance proxies from SPX log returns. We already used realized variance in Project 5, so the definition doesn’t need to be repeated too much. The new point is that realized variance becomes a path whose roughness we estimate.

The daily log return is:

\[ r_t = \log S_t - \log S_{t-1} \]

A one-day realized variance proxy is:

\[ RV_t^{1d} = r_t^2 \]

This is extremely noisy because one squared return is a very weak estimate of the latent variance on that day. So we also use short rolling sums and averages:

\[ RV_t^{(m)} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1} r_{t-j}^2 \]

where \(m\) can be 5, 10, or 21 trading days. The code uses the 10-day proxy as the main realized-variance path:

\[ v_t = RV_t^{(10)} \]

and then works with log variance:

\[ X_t = \log(v_t) \]

The log transformation is useful because variance is positive and very skewed. Log variance compresses huge volatility spikes and makes changes closer to additive. When we later compute increments \(X_{t+\ell}-X_t\), we are asking how the log variance state moves across different lags.

For example, a move from annualized volatility 10% to 20% is a much bigger variance move than it looks in volatility units:

\[ 0.20^2 - 0.10^2 = 0.03 \]

but in log variance it becomes a more balanced relative movement:

\[ \log(0.20^2) - \log(0.10^2) = \log(4) \]

That relative interpretation is closer to how volatility shocks behave: a volatility doubling is meaningful whether it happens from 8% to 16% or from 20% to 40%.

2.1 Variance Proxy Design

The choice of variance proxy matters more in this project than in most earlier notebooks. In a GARCH or HAR forecast, a noisy realized variance proxy can still be useful if the model smooths it. For roughness estimation, the proxy directly changes the slope of the scaling regression.

A one-day squared return is:

\[ RV_t^{1d} = r_t^2 \]

This is unbiased in a loose daily sense if the return has conditional variance \(v_t\), but it has huge noise:

\[ r_t^2 = v_t \varepsilon_t^2 \]

where \(\varepsilon_t^2\) can be very small or very large even when \(v_t\) is unchanged. Taking logs makes this worse when \(r_t^2\) is near zero:

\[ \log(r_t^2) \]

can jump to very negative values because the return happened to be tiny. This is why the raw daily squared-return proxy has weak scaling quality.

A rolling \(m\)-day proxy reduces noise:

\[ RV_t^{(m)} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}r_{t-j}^2 \]

but introduces overlap. For example, \(RV_t^{(10)}\) and \(RV_{t+1}^{(10)}\) share 9 of 10 squared returns. This creates mechanical persistence. If we estimate roughness from a heavily overlapping proxy, we may estimate smoothness created by the estimator rather than the latent variance process.

We handle this by showing multiple proxies and adding non-overlapping block checks. This is the right statistical attitude: roughness estimation is sensitive, so we don’t trust one proxy blindly.

A compact way to think about the bias tradeoff is:

\[ \text{raw daily proxy} = \text{low smoothing} + \text{high noise} \]

\[ \text{rolling proxy} = \text{lower noise} + \text{overlap-induced persistence} \]

The useful estimate is the one that remains plausible after comparing both sides.

3) Fractional Brownian Motion as the Roughness Benchmark

To understand rough volatility, we first need a clean mathematical object that can represent different levels of path roughness. That object is fractional Brownian motion, written \(B_t^H\).

A fractional Brownian motion is a centered Gaussian process with covariance:

\[ \mathbb{E}[B_t^H B_s^H] = \frac{1}{2}\left(t^{2H} + s^{2H} - |t-s|^{2H}\right) \]

The parameter \(H \in (0,1)\) controls the path regularity.

The variance of an increment is:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(B_{t+\Delta}^H - B_t^H) = \Delta^{2H} \]

So the typical size of the increment is:

\[ |B_{t+\Delta}^H - B_t^H| \sim \Delta^H \]

This is the power-law scaling we care about. Brownian motion is the special case \(H = 1/2\) because Brownian increments scale like \(\sqrt{\Delta}\).

The sign of increment dependence also changes with \(H\):

  • \(H < 1/2\): increments are anti-persistent. A positive increment is more likely to be followed by a negative one. The path looks jagged and choppy.
  • \(H = 1/2\): increments are independent. This is ordinary Brownian motion.
  • \(H > 1/2\): increments are persistent. Positive increments tend to be followed by positive increments. The path looks smoother and more trend-like.

The covariance of fractional Gaussian noise, which is the increment process of fBM, is:

\[ \gamma_H(k) = \frac{1}{2}\left(|k+1|^{2H} - 2|k|^{2H} + |k-1|^{2H}\right) \]

Here \(k\) is the lag between increments. This formula is what the simulation uses to build the covariance matrix of increments. Once the covariance matrix is known, the simulation draws correlated Gaussian increments using a Cholesky factorization:

\[ \Delta B^H = LZ \]

where \(Z\) is a vector of independent standard normal draws and \(LL^\top\) is the fractional Gaussian noise covariance matrix. After that we cumulatively sum the increments to obtain the fBM path.

The Cholesky approach is easy to understand and accurate for teaching, although it becomes expensive for large simulations because the covariance matrix is dense. That cost is one reason rough models become computationally heavier than classical Markovian models.

3.1 Path Regularity by Scale

The easiest way to understand \(H\) is to compare the size of increments at a small time scale. Suppose we shrink the time step from one month to one day. If the step size is roughly divided by 21, the typical increment scales by:

\[ 21^{-H} \]

For \(H=0.5\):

\[ 21^{-0.5} \approx 0.218 \]

For \(H=0.1\):

\[ 21^{-0.1} \approx 0.738 \]

This is the whole rough-volatility intuition in one calculation. Under Brownian scaling, daily increments are much smaller than monthly increments. Under rough scaling with \(H=0.1\), the daily increment is still a large fraction of the monthly increment. The process remains jagged when we zoom in.

That is exactly what the option market cares about. A short-dated option is highly sensitive to what happens over the next few days. If volatility can move violently on short horizons, then short-maturity skew should be steep. Classical Heston can generate stochastic variance, but its short-time regularity is tied to ordinary Brownian diffusion. Rough models change the short-time scaling itself.

There is also a common confusion between roughness and long memory. Low \(H\) in rough volatility doesn’t mean volatility is a slow, persistent long-memory process. It means local paths are irregular. A process can look highly persistent in autocorrelation tests because it has clustered volatility, but its local regularity can still be rough. So we focus on increment scaling rather than only autocorrelation.

3.2 Moment Scaling on Simulated Paths

The moment-scaling method estimates \(H\) from how increments behave across lags. For a process \(X_t\), define the \(q\)-moment of increments at lag \(\ell\):

\[ m_q(\ell) = \mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t+\ell} - X_t|^q\right] \]

For fractional Brownian motion, increments scale as \(\ell^H\), so the \(q\)-moment scales as:

\[ m_q(\ell) \propto \ell^{qH} \]

Taking logs gives a line:

\[ \log m_q(\ell) = a_q + qH \log \ell \]

where \(a_q\) is a constant that depends on \(q\) but not on the lag. This is the core estimation equation. For each \(q\), we regress \(\log m_q(\ell)\) on \(\log \ell\):

\[ \log m_q(\ell) = \beta_{0,q} + \beta_{1,q}\log \ell + \varepsilon_{\ell,q} \]

Then:

\[ \widehat{H}_q = \frac{\widehat{\beta}_{1,q}}{q} \]

If the process really behaves like fractional Brownian motion over the tested scales, then \(\widehat{H}_q\) should be fairly stable across different \(q\) values.

For example, if \(q=2\) and the regression slope is \(0.20\), then:

\[ \widehat{H} = \frac{0.20}{2} = 0.10 \]

That means a four-times larger lag increases the typical squared increment by approximately \(4^{0.20}\) rather than by \(4^1\) as we would see for a much smoother process in variance units. The power law is the signal.

3.3 The Meaning of \(H\) in Volatility Modeling

The Hurst exponent is the number that controls how quickly increments shrink as we look at shorter time intervals. For a process \(X_t\), the scaling idea is:

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t+\Delta}-X_t|^q\right] \propto \Delta^{qH} \]

Each part has a role:

  • \(X_{t+\Delta}-X_t\) is the move over a time step of length \(\Delta\),
  • \(q\) chooses which moment we measure,
  • \(H\) controls the slope of the log-log scaling relation,
  • the proportionality sign means we care about the power-law slope, not the exact level.

Taking logs gives a regression-style expression:

\[ \log \mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t+\Delta}-X_t|^q\right] = a_q + qH\log \Delta \]

So if we plot log-moment against log-time-step, the slope should be close to \(qH\). Dividing the slope by \(q\) gives an estimate of \(H\).

For Brownian motion, \(H=0.5\). That means typical increments shrink like \(\sqrt{\Delta}\). If we cut the time interval from one day to one quarter of a day, the typical move is cut roughly in half. For rough volatility, \(H<0.5\). This means the path is less smooth at short horizons. Very small time intervals can still contain relatively sharp changes.

This matters for option pricing because short-maturity options are almost entirely about short-horizon behavior. A one-week option doesn’t care much about the average volatility over the next two years. It cares about whether variance can jump or roughen over the next few trading days. If volatility has \(H=0.1\), the short-term variance path is much more jagged than a Brownian volatility path with \(H=0.5\). That jaggedness is one reason rough models can generate steeper short-end smiles and skews.

There is also a practical warning. Estimated \(H\) depends heavily on the object we measure. Raw squared returns are noisy. Rolling realized variance is smoother but overlap can inflate persistence. Option-implied skew measures risk-neutral roughness, not historical realized roughness. So we shouldn’t expect one universal \(H\) number to appear in every diagnostic. The project uses several estimates because each estimate tells us something different about the volatility process.

The regression form also gives a visual diagnostic. If we plot \(\log \widehat m_q(\ell)\) against \(\log \ell\), a power law appears as a straight line. The slope of that line is \(qH\).

The steps are:

  1. choose a moment order \(q\),
  2. choose lags \(\ell \in \{1,2,4,8,16,32\}\),
  3. compute increments at each lag,
  4. compute the average absolute \(q\)-power of those increments,
  5. take logs,
  6. fit a line.

For a single \(q\):

\[ \widehat{\beta}_{1,q} = \frac{\sum_\ell (\log \ell - \overline{\log \ell})(\log \widehat m_q(\ell)-\overline{\log \widehat m_q})}{\sum_\ell(\log \ell-\overline{\log \ell})^2} \]

and:

\[ \widehat H_q = \frac{\widehat{\beta}_{1,q}}{q} \]

The \(R^2\) of this regression matters. A nice-looking \(H\) estimate with poor \(R^2\) is not useful. It means the power-law relationship is weak. So we report both \(H\) and \(R^2\). We need the estimate and the quality of the scaling relationship.

For example, if \(q=1\) gives \(H=0.12\) with \(R^2=0.98\), and \(q=2\) gives \(H=0.13\) with \(R^2=0.97\), that is strong evidence of stable rough scaling. If \(q=1\) gives \(H=0.02\) with \(R^2=0.10\), that is just noise.

def fgn_covariance(h, n):
    k = np.arange(int(n), dtype=float)
    return 0.5 * (np.abs(k + 1.0) ** (2.0 * h) - 2.0 * np.abs(k) ** (2.0 * h) + np.abs(k - 1.0) ** (2.0 * h))


def fbm_cholesky(h, n_steps, n_paths, seed):
    cov = fgn_covariance(float(h), int(n_steps))
    idx = np.abs(np.subtract.outer(np.arange(int(n_steps)), np.arange(int(n_steps))))
    mat = cov[idx] + 1e-12 * np.eye(int(n_steps))
    z = np.random.default_rng(int(seed)).standard_normal((int(n_steps), int(n_paths)))
    increments = np.linalg.cholesky(mat) @ z / (int(n_steps) ** float(h))
    paths = np.vstack([np.zeros((1, int(n_paths))), np.cumsum(increments, axis=0)])
    return pd.DataFrame(paths, index=np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, int(n_steps) + 1))


def path_moment_scaling(x, q_values, lags):
    values = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)

    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

4) Estimating Roughness from SPX Realized Variance

Now we apply the same scaling logic to SPX log realized variance. The object is:

\[ X_t = \log RV_t \]

and the increments are:

\[ \Delta_\ell X_t = X_{t+\ell} - X_t \]

For each moment order \(q\) and each lag \(\ell\), we estimate:

\[ \widehat{m}_q(\ell) = \frac{1}{N_\ell}\sum_t |\Delta_\ell X_t|^q \]

Then we fit:

\[ \log \widehat{m}_q(\ell) = \beta_{0,q} + \beta_{1,q}\log \ell + e_{\ell,q} \]

and use:

\[ \widehat{H}_q = \frac{\widehat{\beta}_{1,q}}{q} \]

There are two implementation details that matter here.

First, the code estimates \(H\) separately for different realized-variance proxies: raw daily squared returns, 5-day rolling RV, 10-day rolling RV, and 21-day rolling RV. This helps separate true roughness from measurement noise. A one-day squared return is too noisy and can make the scaling estimate meaningless. Longer rolling RV proxies are smoother, but overlapping windows can artificially increase persistence.

Second, the code also uses a pooled regression. Instead of estimating \(H\) separately for each \(q\), the pooled version stacks all moment orders and estimates one shared \(H\):

\[ \log \widehat{m}_q(\ell) = a_q + Hq\log \ell + e_{q,\ell} \]

Here each \(q\) gets its own intercept \(a_q\), but the slope coefficient is constrained to be \(Hq\). In matrix form, if each row is one pair \((q,\ell)\), the first column of the design matrix is \(q\log \ell\):

\[ y = X\theta + e \]

with:

\[ y_{q,\ell} = \log \widehat{m}_q(\ell), \qquad X_{q,\ell,1} = q\log \ell \]

The coefficient on this first column is the pooled estimate of \(H\). This is useful because individual \(q\) estimates can be noisy, especially at high moment orders where extreme volatility jumps dominate the moment.

4.1 Separate-\(q\) and Pooled-\(q\) Estimation

The separate-\(q\) estimator is easy to read:

\[ \widehat H_q = \frac{\widehat\beta_{1,q}}{q} \]

but every \(q\) sees the data differently. Low \(q\) values treat ordinary fluctuations more evenly. High \(q\) values give much more weight to extreme volatility jumps. In financial variance data, \(q=3\) can be strongly influenced by crisis observations.

The pooled estimator tries to stabilize this by estimating one \(H\) across all \(q\) values:

\[ \log \widehat m_q(\ell) = a_q + Hq\log \ell + e_{q,\ell} \]

The intercept \(a_q\) is allowed to change with \(q\) because the level of the moment is different for \(q=0.5\), \(q=1\), or \(q=2\). But the slope structure is constrained: the lag slope must be proportional to \(q\).

This is a simple but powerful design. In matrix notation, we can write:

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \]

where the first coefficient of \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is \(H\). A row corresponding to moment order \(q_i\) and lag \(\ell_i\) has:

\[ X_{i,1}=q_i\log \ell_i \]

and then dummy/intercept columns for each \(q\). The pooled OLS estimate is:

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y} \]

We also use a robust option when available. The reason is that volatility data has extreme jumps. A Huber-style regression reduces the influence of huge observations without throwing them away entirely. That fits the financial problem: crisis spikes are real, but we don’t want one crash week to determine the roughness exponent alone.

def power_variation(x, q_values, lags):
    s = pd.Series(x).dropna()
    values = s.to_numpy(dtype=float)
    rows = []
    for q in q_values:
        for lag in lags:
            lag = int(lag)
            inc = values[lag:] - values[:-lag]
            inc = inc[np.isfinite(inc)]
            moment = float(np.mean(np.abs(inc) ** float(q)))
            rows.append({"q": float(q), "lag": lag, "moment": moment, "log_lag": np.log(lag), "log_moment": np.log(moment), "n": int(inc.size)})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


def hurst_ols(scaling):
    rows = []
    for q, g in scaling.groupby("q", sort=True):
        x = g["log_lag"].to_numpy(dtype=float)
        y = g["log_moment"].to_numpy(dtype=float)
        design = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), x])
        beta, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(design, y, rcond=None)
        fitted = design @ beta
        resid = y - fitted
        ss_res = float(np.sum(resid * resid))
        ss_tot = float(np.sum((y - np.mean(y)) ** 2))
        sigma2 = ss_res / max(len(x) - 2, 1)
        cov = sigma2 * np.linalg.pinv(design.T @ design)
        rows.append({"q": float(q), "slope": float(beta[1]), "h": float(beta[1] / q), "standard_error": float(np.sqrt(max(cov[1, 1], 0.0))), "r2": float(1.0 - ss_res / ss_tot), "n": int(len(g))})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


def hurst_pooled(scaling, q_exclude=(3.0,), use_huber=True):
    data = scaling.copy()
    data["log_lag_fit"] = np.log(pd.to_numeric(data["lag"], errors="coerce").replace(0, np.nan))
    data["log_moment_fit"] = np.log(pd.to_numeric(data["moment"], errors="coerce").replace([0, np.inf, -np.inf], np.nan))
    data = data.replace([np.inf, -np.inf], np.nan).dropna(subset=["q", "log_lag_fit", "log_moment_fit"])
    if q_exclude:
        data = data[~data["q"].isin(q_exclude)].copy()
    if len(data) < 4:
        return {"H": np.nan, "se": np.nan, "r2": np.nan, "n": len(data), "method": "pooled"}
    q_unique = sorted(data["q"].unique())
    n, n_q = len(data), len(q_unique)
    q_idx = {q: i for i, q in enumerate(q_unique)}
    X = np.zeros((n, 1 + n_q), dtype=float)
    q_arr = data["q"].to_numpy(float)
    ll_arr = data["log_lag_fit"].to_numpy(float)
    X[:, 0] = q_arr * ll_arr
    for i, rq in enumerate(q_arr):
        X[i, 1 + q_idx[rq]] = 1.0
    y = data["log_moment_fit"].to_numpy(float)
    h_est, se, r2, method_used = np.nan, np.nan, np.nan, "ols"
    if use_huber:
        try:
            from sklearn.linear_model import HuberRegressor
            mdl = HuberRegressor(epsilon=1.35, max_iter=500, fit_intercept=False)
            mdl.fit(X, y)
            h_est = float(mdl.coef_[0])
            y_hat = mdl.predict(X)
            ss_res = float(np.sum((y - y_hat) ** 2))
            ss_tot = float(np.sum((y - np.mean(y)) ** 2))
            r2 = float(1.0 - ss_res / ss_tot) if ss_tot > 0 else np.nan
            method_used = "huber"
        except Exception:
            use_huber = False
    if not use_huber or not np.isfinite(h_est):
        beta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
        h_est = float(beta[0])
        y_hat = X @ beta
        ss_res = float(np.sum((y - y_hat) ** 2))
        ss_tot = float(np.sum((y - np.mean(y)) ** 2))
        r2 = float(1.0 - ss_res / ss_tot) if ss_tot > 0 else np.nan
        dof = max(n - 1 - n_q, 1)
        sigma2 = ss_res / dof
        try:
            cov = sigma2 * np.linalg.pinv(X.T @ X)
            se = float(np.sqrt(max(cov[0, 0], 0.0)))
        except Exception:
            se = np.nan
        method_used = "ols"
    return {"H": h_est, "se": se, "r2": r2, "n": n, "method": method_used}


def hurst_multiscale(returns, windows=(1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 21), q_values=(0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0), lags=(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32), eps=1e-12, use_huber=True):
    rv_1d = (returns * returns).clip(lower=float(eps))
    rows = []
    for w in windows:
        w = int(w)
        if w == 1:
            log_rv = np.log(rv_1d.clip(lower=float(eps))).rename(f"log_rv_{w}d")
        else:
            rv_w = rv_1d.rolling(w).sum() / float(w)
            log_rv = np.log(rv_w.dropna().clip(lower=float(eps))).rename(f"log_rv_{w}d")
        sc = power_variation(log_rv, list(q_values), list(lags))
        h_per_q = hurst_ols(sc)
        h_pooled = hurst_pooled(sc, use_huber=use_huber)
        h_q1 = float(h_per_q[h_per_q["q"].between(0.9, 1.1)]["h"].mean()) if not h_per_q.empty else np.nan
        rows.append({
            "window_days": w,
            "n_obs": int(log_rv.shape[0]),

    return df

  • rolling realized variance is cleaner but can over-smooth,
  • non-overlapping checks help detect artificial persistence,
  • option-implied skew gives a separate risk-neutral roughness estimate.

This is also why we shouldn’t use \(H\) like a magic constant. Roughness estimation is an inference problem with measurement error. The model is only as reliable as the diagnostics around the estimate.

5) Rough-Kernel Forecasting

After estimating roughness from realized variance, we use it in a forecasting model. This is the physical-measure side of the project: we ask whether a rough-memory kernel can help forecast future realized variance.

The rough-kernel forecast is built from past realized variance values:

\[ \widehat{v}_{t,h} = \sum_{j=1}^{L} w_{j,h}(H)v_{t-j+1} \]

where:

  • \(v_{t-j+1}\) is a past daily variance proxy,
  • \(L\) is the lookback length,
  • \(h\) is the forecast horizon,
  • \(w_{j,h}(H)\) is the weight assigned to that lag.

The code uses horizon-adjusted power-law weights:

\[ w_{j,h}(H) = \frac{(j+h)^{H+1/2} - j^{H+1/2}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left[(\ell+h)^{H+1/2} - \ell^{H+1/2}\right]} \]

These weights are positive and normalized:

\[ \sum_{j=1}^{L} w_{j,h}(H) = 1 \]

The finance meaning is clear: we forecast future variance as a weighted average of past variance, but the weights decay in a fractional way rather than an exponential way. GARCH-like models usually rely on recursive exponential-type updating. HAR-RV uses a few block averages such as daily, weekly, and monthly variance. The rough kernel uses a smoother continuum of memory weights.

The estimated \(H\) changes the shape of the weights. When \(H\) is low, the near past receives more weight, but the decay isn’t just a short exponential cutoff. The model still keeps a long fractional tail. That is the rough-volatility compromise: volatility reacts sharply at short horizons while still carrying memory across longer horizons.

The evaluation uses variance losses, not just volatility errors. A key loss is QLIKE:

\[ QLIKE(v,\widehat{v}) = \frac{v}{\widehat{v}} - \log\left(\frac{v}{\widehat{v}}\right) - 1 \]

where \(v\) is realized variance and \(\widehat{v}\) is forecast variance. Lower is better. QLIKE is useful because it penalizes variance forecasts in a scale-aware way and is commonly used for volatility-forecast evaluation.

5.1 Relation to HAR and GARCH

The comparison models are reused from Project 5, so we don’t need to re-derive them deeply. But it helps to understand what the rough kernel is competing against.

HAR-RV uses a small set of block averages:

\[ RV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_d RV_t^{(d)} + \beta_w RV_t^{(w)} + \beta_m RV_t^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{t+1} \]

It approximates heterogeneous market horizons: daily traders, weekly traders, and monthly investors. It is simple, stable, and very hard to beat.

GARCH updates variance recursively:

\[ \sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \alpha r_t^2 + \beta \sigma_t^2 \]

This is a compact exponential-memory model. Shocks decay through the \(\beta\) term. Extensions such as GJR and EGARCH add asymmetry and log-variance behavior, but the basic idea is still recursive updating.

The rough kernel takes a different route. It doesn’t use a few block averages and doesn’t use a one-step recursion. It uses many lags with a power-law memory shape:

\[ \widehat v_{t,h} = \sum_{j=1}^{L}w_{j,h}(H)v_{t-j+1} \]

If HAR is a three-bucket approximation and GARCH is an exponential-recursion approximation, the rough kernel is a fractional-memory approximation. This makes it a natural forecast model after we estimate \(H\).

def kernel_weights(h, lookback):
    j = np.arange(1, int(lookback) + 1, dtype=float)
    weights = j ** (float(h) - 0.5)
    weights = weights / weights.sum()
    return weights[::-1]


def kernel_weights_horizon(h, lookback, horizon):
    j = np.arange(1, int(lookback) + 1, dtype=float)
    weights = (j + float(horizon)) ** (float(h) + 0.5) - j ** (float(h) + 0.5)
    weights = np.maximum(weights, 0.0)
    weights = weights / weights.sum()
    return weights[::-1]


def kernel_forecasts(rv, targets, h, horizons, train_window, signal_step, annualization):
    v = pd.Series(rv).dropna().clip(lower=1e-12)
    values = v.to_numpy(dtype=float)
    records = []
    for pos in range(int(train_window), len(v) - int(max(horizons)), int(signal_step)):
        hist = values[pos - int(train_window) + 1:pos + 1]
        if len(hist) != int(train_window):
            continue
        for horizon in horizons:
            horizon = int(horizon)
            weights = kernel_weights_horizon(h, train_window, horizon)
            forecast_daily = float(np.sum(hist * weights))
            realized_sum = float(targets.loc[v.index[pos], f"realized_var_sum_{horizon}"])
            records.append({
                "date": v.index[pos],
                "model": "rough_kernel",
                "horizon": horizon,
                "forecast_var_daily": forecast_daily,
                "forecast_var_sum": forecast_daily * horizon,
                "forecast_var_ann": annualization * forecast_daily,
                "forecast_vol_ann": np.sqrt(annualization * forecast_daily),
                "realized_var_sum": realized_sum,
                "realized_var_ann": annualization * realized_sum / horizon,
                "realized_vol_ann": np.sqrt(annualization * realized_sum / horizon) if realized_sum >= 0 else np.nan,
            })
    return pd.DataFrame(records)

\[ RMSE_v = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_t(\widehat v_t-v_t)^2} \]

\[ MAE_v = \frac{1}{N}\sum_t|\widehat v_t-v_t| \]

6) Option Chain Cleaning and Implied-Volatility Surface Support

We now move from realized variance to option-implied information. The data and basic IV extraction are repeated from Projects 4 and 8, so we keep the discussion short. The important point is that rough volatility is tested through the short-maturity skew of the implied-volatility surface.

The cleaned option chain must support three things:

  1. enough quotes across dates,
  2. enough expiries per date,
  3. enough moneyness range around the money to estimate ATM skew.

The moneyness coordinate is log moneyness:

\[ k = \log\left(\frac{K}{S}\right) \]

The implied-volatility surface is written as:

\[ \sigma_{\text{imp}} = \sigma_{\text{imp}}(k,\tau) \]

where \(\tau\) is time to maturity in years. We already built surfaces in Project 8, but here we use the surface for a different reason. Project 8 used the surface mainly for smooth interpolation, local volatility, and surface diagnostics. Here we need a smooth enough surface to estimate the derivative:

\[ \partial_k \sigma_{\text{imp}}(0,\tau) \]

That derivative is the ATM skew at maturity \(\tau\). If the surface is noisy, the skew estimate becomes noisy too. So quote cleaning and surface smoothing are not decorative preprocessing steps; they directly determine the stability of the roughness estimate from options.

6.1 From Clean Quotes to Surface Targets

The cleaning steps create a path from raw option quotes to surface-ready implied volatilities:

\[ \text{raw quotes} \rightarrow \text{clean prices} \rightarrow \text{forward/rate/carry} \rightarrow \text{IV} \rightarrow \text{surface fit} \]

The forward is inferred from put-call parity where possible:

\[ C - P = D(F-K) \]

so:

\[ F = K + \frac{C-P}{D} \]

Once the forward and discount factor are available, the Black-76 implied volatility solves:

\[ C^{\text{Black76}}(F,K,\tau,\sigma,D) = C^{\text{market}} \]

We already solved IV in Project 4, but the rough-volatility use is stricter. A noisy IV quote away from the money may not hurt a broad surface visualization, but it can distort skew derivatives and calibration residuals. That is why the code filters extreme IV values, low weights, and poor quote regions before surface fitting.

The option-surface section also prepares calibration grids. Instead of calibrating to every raw quote directly, we build a controlled surface target grid across moneyness and maturity. This makes the model comparison more balanced: Heston and rough Heston are evaluated across comparable regions rather than being dominated by whatever strikes happen to have the most raw quotes.

7) Smooth Surface Fit and ATM Skew Power Law

We fit two versions of the surface: a visual/smoothed fit and a more regularized version for derivative-sensitive tasks. We don’t need to re-teach spline surfaces here. The key point is that ATM skew is a derivative, and derivatives amplify noise. A small wiggle in implied volatility can become a large error in \(\partial_k \sigma\).

The ATM skew at maturity \(\tau\) is estimated with a centered finite difference:

\[ \psi(\tau) = \partial_k \sigma_{\text{imp}}(0,\tau) \approx \frac{\sigma_{\text{imp}}(+\Delta k,\tau) - \sigma_{\text{imp}}(-\Delta k,\tau)}{2\Delta k} \]

In equity indices, \(\psi(\tau)\) is usually negative. The implied-volatility smile is higher for downside strikes, so as \(k\) increases from negative to positive, IV tends to fall.

Rough-volatility theory gives a short-maturity power law:

\[ |\psi(\tau)| \approx c \tau^{H - 1/2} \]

Since \(H < 1/2\), the exponent \(H - 1/2\) is negative. That means the skew becomes steeper as maturity goes to zero.

The code fits this in the form:

\[ |\psi(\tau)| \approx c \tau^{-\alpha} \]

Taking logs gives:

\[ \log |\psi(\tau)| = \log c - \alpha \log \tau \]

So the fitted roughness estimate is:

\[ \widehat{H} = \frac{1}{2} - \widehat{\alpha} \]

For example, if the fitted slope gives \(\alpha = 0.25\), then:

\[ \widehat{H} = 0.50 - 0.25 = 0.25 \]

This is an option-implied estimate of roughness. It doesn’t have to match the realized-variance estimate exactly because it is risk-neutral, maturity-smoothed, and extracted from option prices rather than historical returns.

7.1 Short-Maturity Skew as a Roughness Fingerprint

The ATM skew power law is one of the main reasons rough volatility became so important in option pricing. Classical diffusive stochastic-volatility models can generate skew, but their short-time asymptotics usually don’t produce the same steep power-law explosion.

The rough-volatility relationship can be written:

\[ \psi(\tau) = \partial_k \sigma_{\text{imp}}(0,\tau) \]

and for small \(\tau\):

\[ |\psi(\tau)| \approx c\tau^{H-\frac{1}{2}} \]

If \(H=0.5\), then:

\[ H-\frac{1}{2}=0 \]

so the short-end skew is roughly flat in maturity. If \(H=0.1\), then:

\[ H-\frac{1}{2}=-0.4 \]

so the skew behaves like:

\[ |\psi(\tau)| \approx c\tau^{-0.4} \]

Now compare two maturities, 7 days and 28 days. The maturity ratio is 4. With \(H=0.1\), the skew ratio is approximately:

\[ \left(\frac{7}{28}\right)^{-0.4} = 4^{0.4} \approx 1.74 \]

So the 7-day skew can be about 74% steeper than the 28-day skew under this rough scaling. That kind of short-end steepness is hard to ignore in equity-index options.

The code fits \(\alpha\) in:

\[ |\psi(\tau)| = c\tau^{-\alpha} \]

and then maps:

\[ H = \frac{1}{2} - \alpha \]

This mapping is the bridge from a market-observable surface derivative to a roughness parameter.

The skew-window choice is not just a coding detail. Very short maturities can be noisy because of microstructure, pin risk, expiry effects, and low vega. Long maturities can dilute the rough short-time signature because the asymptotic relation is about \(\tau \to 0\).

So we test several windows, such as 7–45 days, 7–60 days, 14–90 days, and 14–150 days. If the estimated \(H\) changes drastically across windows, the power-law evidence is unstable. If the estimate is consistent and the \(R^2\) is high, the skew roughness is more trustworthy.

def atm_skew(q, fit, tau_values, dk=0.01):
    rows = []
    for tau in tau_values:
        left = float(surface_iv(fit, np.array([-dk]), np.array([tau]))[0])
        right = float(surface_iv(fit, np.array([dk]), np.array([tau]))[0])
        atm = float(surface_iv(fit, np.array([0.0]), np.array([tau]))[0])
        rows.append({"tau": float(tau), "tau_days": float(tau * ann_days), "atm_iv": atm, "atm_skew": (right - left) / (2.0 * dk), "n": int(len(q[np.isclose(q["tau"], tau, atol=3 / ann_days)]))})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


def power_law(psi, tau_min_days=None, tau_max_days=None, window=""):
    q = psi.replace([np.inf, -np.inf], np.nan).dropna(subset=["tau", "atm_skew"]).copy()
    if tau_min_days is not None:
        q = q[q["tau_days"].ge(float(tau_min_days))].copy()
    if tau_max_days is not None:
        q = q[q["tau_days"].le(float(tau_max_days))].copy()
    q = q[(q["tau"] > 0) & (q["atm_skew"].abs() > 0)]
    if len(q) < 3:
        return pd.DataFrame([{"window": window, "c": np.nan, "alpha": np.nan, "h_from_skew": np.nan, "r2": np.nan, "n": int(len(q))}])
    x = np.log(q["tau"].to_numpy(dtype=float))
    y = np.log(q["atm_skew"].abs().to_numpy(dtype=float))
    design = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), x])
    beta, _, _, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(design, y, rcond=None)
    fitted = design @ beta
    ss_res = float(np.sum((y - fitted) ** 2))
    ss_tot = float(np.sum((y - y.mean()) ** 2))
    alpha = -float(beta[1])
    return pd.DataFrame([{"window": window, "c": float(np.exp(beta[0])), "alpha": alpha, "h_from_skew": 0.5 - alpha, "r2": 1.0 - ss_res / ss_tot, "n": int(len(q))}])

8) Heston as the Markovian Control Model

Before going into rough models, we fit Heston as the classical stochastic-volatility control model. Heston was already explained in Project 11, so here we only need the structure that matters for comparison.

Under a risk-neutral measure, the Heston model is:

\[ dS_t = (r-q)S_t\,dt + \sqrt{V_t}S_t\,dW_t^S \]

\[ dV_t = \kappa(\theta - V_t)\,dt + \sigma_v\sqrt{V_t}\,dW_t^V \]

with:

\[ d\langle W^S, W^V\rangle_t = \rho\,dt \]

The parameters mean:

  • \(V_0\): current instantaneous variance,
  • \(\kappa\): speed of mean reversion,
  • \(\theta\): long-run variance level,
  • \(\sigma_v\): volatility of variance,
  • \(\rho\): leverage correlation between spot shocks and variance shocks.

The model is Markovian because the state at time \(t\) is just \((S_t,V_t)\). Once we know those two values, the future distribution doesn’t need the full path history. This makes Heston fast and elegant, especially with Fourier/COS pricing from Project 14. But that Markovian structure also limits the short-time skew behavior. A finite-dimensional diffusion has a different short-maturity slope shape from rough-volatility models.

The calibration objective is a weighted price error:

\[ \min_\Theta \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\left(C_i^{\text{model}}(\Theta) - C_i^{\text{market}}\right)^2 \]

where \(\Theta=(V_0,\kappa,\theta,\sigma_v,\rho)\), and the weights account for quote quality and scale. We use Heston as a disciplined baseline: if rough models can’t improve on it in the right places, then their extra complexity isn’t justified.

8.1 Parameter Identifiability in Heston

Heston calibration is famous for parameter interaction. Different parameter sets can produce similar option surfaces, especially if the calibration window is short or the available maturities are limited.

For example:

  • increasing \(\sigma_v\) can steepen skew and increase curvature,
  • making \(\rho\) more negative also steepens downside skew,
  • changing \(\kappa\) and \(\theta\) can shift how variance term structure behaves,
  • \(V_0\) mainly controls the very short end.

This means a calibrated Heston parameter is not always a clean economic estimate. It is partly a coordinate system for fitting the surface.

A useful way to think about Heston parameter roles is:

\[ \text{level} \approx V_0,\theta \]

\[ \text{term structure} \approx \kappa,\theta,V_0 \]

\[ \text{skew} \approx \rho,\sigma_v \]

\[ \text{curvature} \approx \sigma_v,\rho,\kappa \]

The rough-Heston model adds \(H\), which affects short-time regularity and skew scaling. This can reduce pressure on \(\rho\) and \(\sigma_v\) in some regions, but it also adds another dimension of calibration risk. So the comparison has to use diagnostics, not just parameter stories.

9) Forward Variance Curve and Rough Bergomi

Rough Bergomi is the first rough pricing model we use. It starts from the forward variance curve rather than from a single variance state. This is a big conceptual shift.

The forward variance curve is:

\[ \xi_0(t) = \mathbb{E}_0[V_t] \]

It is the market-implied expected future variance at each horizon. We estimate it from the ATM total variance curve:

\[ w(\tau) = \tau \sigma_{\text{ATM}}^2(\tau) \]

A practical approximation is:

\[ \xi_0(\tau) \approx \frac{\partial w(\tau)}{\partial \tau} \]

The code computes this from the smooth surface by taking ATM implied volatility across maturities, converting it to total variance, and differentiating/interpolating carefully.

Rough Bergomi then models variance as a lognormal rough process around this forward variance curve:

\[ V_t = \xi_0(t)\exp\left(\nu W_t^H - \frac{1}{2}\nu^2 t^{2H}\right) \]

The fractional driver is usually built from a Brownian motion through a kernel:

\[ W_t^H = \sqrt{2H}\int_0^t (t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\,dW_s^V \]

The spot process is:

\[ dS_t = (r-q)S_t\,dt + \sqrt{V_t}S_t\,dW_t^S \]

with correlation:

\[ d\langle W^S,W^V\rangle_t = \rho\,dt \]

The parameters are:

  • \(H\): roughness of volatility,
  • \(\nu\): vol-of-vol, controlling how strongly variance fluctuates around \(\xi_0(t)\),
  • \(\rho\): spot-volatility correlation, controlling the leverage effect and smile skew.

A more negative \(\rho\) usually creates a steeper equity-style downside skew. A larger \(\nu\) increases smile curvature and variance uncertainty. A smaller \(H\) makes the short-end skew steeper because variance reacts in a rougher, more irregular way near the present.

The calibration uses Monte Carlo simulation, so it is noisier than Heston. We use variance reduction tools such as Sobol-style sampling and antithetic paths where available. Even then, rBergomi is computationally expensive because each option price depends on simulated rough variance paths.

9.1 Forward Variance Extraction

The forward variance curve is one of the most important inputs in rBergomi. The ATM total variance is:

\[ w(\tau)=\tau\sigma^2_{\text{ATM}}(\tau) \]

If total variance is the integral of forward variance,

\[ w(\tau)=\int_0^\tau \xi_0(u)\,du \]

then differentiating gives:

\[ \xi_0(\tau)=\frac{dw(\tau)}{d\tau} \]

This is exactly why the surface fit has to be smooth across maturity. A small wiggle in ATM IV becomes a larger wiggle in total variance derivative. The code uses a dense maturity grid and interpolation so the forward variance curve is positive and stable enough for simulation.

There is also a financial interpretation. \(\xi_0(\tau)\) is the market-implied expectation of instantaneous variance at future horizon \(\tau\). If the curve slopes upward, the market expects future variance to be higher than near-term variance. If it slopes downward, near-term risk is priced above longer-term variance. rBergomi uses this curve as the deterministic backbone, then adds rough random fluctuations around it.

This is a nice separation:

\[ \text{forward variance curve} = \text{term structure level} \]

\[ (H,\nu,\rho) = \text{roughness, variance randomness, skew direction} \]

9.2 Simulating Rough Bergomi Paths

The rBergomi implementation is Monte Carlo based. That means the model doesn’t give one deterministic smile from a closed-form formula. It generates many joint paths for spot and variance, prices the option payoff on those paths, and then converts the average discounted payoff back into implied volatility.

The rough variance path has two moving pieces:

\[ V_t = \xi_0(t)\exp\left(\nu W_t^H - \frac{1}{2}\nu^2 t^{2H}\right) \]

The first part, \(\xi_0(t)\), is deterministic once we extract the forward variance curve from the surface. The second part is random. The term \(\nu W_t^H\) pushes variance up and down, while the correction term \(-\frac{1}{2}\nu^2t^{2H}\) keeps the lognormal multiplier centered correctly. Without that correction, the exponential transformation would bias variance upward because exponentials of normal random variables have mean greater than one.

To see the idea with a simple example, suppose two maturities have the same forward variance backbone, but one simulation path receives a positive rough-vol shock early. Because \(W_t^H\) is rough, that early shock can create a sharp near-term variance burst. A standard smooth-vol model tends to spread that shock more gently. This is exactly why rBergomi can create steep short-maturity smiles: option prices care a lot about what can happen over the next few days, and rough variance can move sharply over those few days.

The spot path uses a correlated Brownian shock:

\[ dS_t = (r-q)S_tdt + \sqrt{V_t}S_tdW_t^S \]

The variance driver and spot driver have correlation \(\rho\). In simulation form, we can think of the spot shock as:

\[ dW_t^S = \rho\,dW_t^V + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,dW_t^\perp \]

where \(dW_t^\perp\) is an independent Brownian shock. This construction makes the spot move partially with the variance shock and partially with independent price noise. If \(\rho<0\), a negative spot move tends to arrive with a positive variance shock. That is the leverage effect behind the equity index skew.

The pricing step is:

\[ C(K,T) = e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}\left[(S_T-K)^+\right] \]

The expectation is approximated by averaging across simulation paths:

\[ \widehat{C}(K,T)=e^{-rT}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(S_T^{(m)}-K)^+ \]

where \(M\) is the number of Monte Carlo paths. The code repeats this for several strikes and maturities, then maps the model prices into implied volatilities so they can be compared to the market surface.

This is why the path convergence table matters. When paths increase from hundreds to thousands, the option price estimate should stabilize. If the implied vol moves too much as path count changes, the model output is still dominated by Monte Carlo noise. The table shows that the rBergomi ATM call estimate moves by a few dollars across path counts, which is acceptable for a teaching implementation but still reminds us that Monte Carlo rough-vol calibration is expensive and noisy.

The important implementation point is that rBergomi is useful for generating the right kind of rough smile shape, but it is not the fastest daily calibration engine. It is path-based, and every parameter evaluation requires many simulated paths.

    dense_tau = dense_tau[dense_tau > 0]
    base = []
    for tau in dense_tau:
        sigma = float(surface_iv(fit, np.array([0.0]), np.array([tau]))[0])
        if np.isfinite(sigma) and sigma > 0:
            base.append({"tau": float(tau), "total_variance": sigma * sigma * float(tau)})
    try:
        from scipy.interpolate import PchipInterpolator
        if len(base) < 3:
            raise ValueError("too few points for PCHIP")
        tv_df = pd.DataFrame(base).sort_values("tau").reset_index(drop=True)
        pchip = PchipInterpolator(tv_df["tau"].values, tv_df["total_variance"].values)
        pchip_d = pchip.derivative()
        out_rows = []
        for tau in tau_arr:
            sigma = float(surface_iv(fit, np.array([0.0]), np.array([tau]))[0])
            fv = max(float(pchip_d(tau)), 1e-6)
            out_rows.append({"tau": float(tau), "total_variance": float(pchip(tau)), "atm_iv": sigma if np.isfinite(sigma) else np.nan, "variance": fv})
        return pd.DataFrame(out_rows)
    except Exception:
        out_rows = []
        last_tau, last_total = 0.0, 0.0
        for tau in tau_arr:
            sigma = float(surface_iv(fit, np.array([0.0]), np.array([tau]))[0])
            total = sigma * sigma * float(tau) if np.isfinite(sigma) and sigma > 0 else last_total

@njit
def rbergomi_xi_interp(t, xi_tau, xi_var):
    if t <= xi_tau[0]:
        return xi_var[0]
    for i in range(1, len(xi_tau)):
        if t <= xi_tau[i]:
            w = (t - xi_tau[i - 1]) / max(xi_tau[i] - xi_tau[i - 1], 1e-12)
            return xi_var[i - 1] * (1.0 - w) + xi_var[i] * w
    return xi_var[len(xi_var) - 1]


@njit
def rbergomi_paths_numba(spot, xi_tau, xi_var, h, nu, rho, rate, dividend_yield, tau, z1, z2):
    n, m = z1.shape
    dt = tau / m
    out_s = np.empty((n, m + 1))
    out_v = np.empty((n, m + 1))
    kernel = np.empty(m)
    rough_var = np.empty(m)
    alpha = h + 0.5
    scale = np.sqrt(max(2.0 * h, 1e-12)) * (dt ** h) / max(alpha, 1e-12)
    for j in range(m):
        kernel[j] = ((j + 1.0) ** alpha - j ** alpha)
    # Precompute actual discrete variance of the rough driver at each step.
    # Using t^(2H) is wrong for finite step counts; the true var is scale^2 * sum(kernel[l]^2).
    for j in range(m):
        rv = 0.0
        for l in range(j + 1):
            rv += kernel[l] * kernel[l]
        rough_var[j] = scale * scale * rv
    for i in range(n):
        out_s[i, 0] = spot
        out_v[i, 0] = rbergomi_xi_interp(0.0, xi_tau, xi_var)
        for j in range(m):
            rough = 0.0
            for l in range(j + 1):
                rough += kernel[l] * z1[i, j - l]
            rough *= scale
            xi_t = rbergomi_xi_interp((j + 1.0) * dt, xi_tau, xi_var)
            v = xi_t * np.exp(nu * rough - 0.5 * nu * nu * rough_var[j])
            if v < 1e-10:
                v = 1e-10
            if v > 25.0:
                v = 25.0
            out_v[i, j + 1] = v
            dw = rho * z1[i, j] + np.sqrt(max(1.0 - rho * rho, 1e-12)) * z2[i, j]
            out_s[i, j + 1] = out_s[i, j] * np.exp((rate - dividend_yield - 0.5 * v) * dt + np.sqrt(v * dt) * dw)
    return out_s, out_v


def _next_pow2(n):
    p = 1
    while p < n:
        p <<= 1
    return p


def _crn(paths, steps, seed, use_sobol=True):
    paths, steps, half = int(paths), int(steps), int(paths) // 2
    z1_h = z2_h = None
    if use_sobol:
        try:
            from scipy.stats.qmc import Sobol
            from scipy.stats import norm as _norm
            n_sobol = _next_pow2(half)
            sob1 = Sobol(d=steps, scramble=True, seed=int(seed))
            sob2 = Sobol(d=steps, scramble=True, seed=int(seed) + 1)
            u1 = sob1.random(n_sobol)[:half]
            u2 = sob2.random(n_sobol)[:half]
            u1 = np.clip(u1, 1e-12, 1.0 - 1e-12)
            u2 = np.clip(u2, 1e-12, 1.0 - 1e-12)
            z1_h = _norm.ppf(u1).astype(float)
            z2_h = _norm.ppf(u2).astype(float)
        except Exception:
            pass
    if z1_h is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng(int(seed))
        z1_h = rng.standard_normal((half, steps))
        z2_h = rng.standard_normal((half, steps))
    return np.vstack([z1_h, -z1_h]), np.vstack([z2_h, -z2_h])


def rbergomi_sim(spot, xi, h, nu, rho, rate, dividend_yield, tau, paths, steps, seed, antithetic=True, z1=None, z2=None):
    if z1 is not None and z2 is not None:
        pass
    else:
        rng = np.random.default_rng(int(seed))
        half = int(paths) // 2 if antithetic else int(paths)
        z1 = rng.standard_normal((half, int(steps)))
        z2 = rng.standard_normal((half, int(steps)))
        if antithetic:
            z1 = np.vstack([z1, -z1])
            z2 = np.vstack([z2, -z2])
    xi_tau = np.asarray(xi["tau"], dtype=float)
    xi_var = np.asarray(xi["variance"], dtype=float)
    return rbergomi_paths_numba(float(spot), xi_tau, xi_var, float(h), float(nu), float(rho), float(rate), float(dividend_yield), float(tau), z1, z2)


def rbergomi_mc_smile(spot, xi, h, nu, rho, rate, dividend_yield, tau, strikes, paths, steps, seed, z1=None, z2=None):
    s, v = rbergomi_sim(spot, xi, h, nu, rho, rate, dividend_yield, tau, paths, steps, seed, antithetic=(z1 is None), z1=z1, z2=z2)
    terminal = s[:, -1]
    discount = np.exp(-float(rate) * float(tau))
    forward = float(spot) * np.exp((float(rate) - float(dividend_yield)) * float(tau))
    n_k = len(strikes)
    prices = np.empty(n_k)
    price_ses = np.empty(n_k)
    ivs = np.full(n_k, np.nan)
    for i, K in enumerate(strikes):
        # OTM pricing: puts for k<0, calls for k>=0 — avoids ITM IV inversion noise
        if K < forward:
            payoff = np.maximum(K - terminal, 0.0)
            px = discount * float(payoff.mean())
            se = discount * float(payoff.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(payoff)))
            iv_arr = implied_vol("put", np.array([px]), forward, np.array([K]), tau, discount, engine="auto")
        else:
            payoff = np.maximum(terminal - K, 0.0)
            px = discount * float(payoff.mean())
            se = discount * float(payoff.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(payoff)))
            iv_arr = implied_vol("call", np.array([px]), forward, np.array([K]), tau, discount, engine="auto")
        prices[i] = px
        price_ses[i] = se
        ivs[i] = float(iv_arr[0]) if len(iv_arr) > 0 and np.isfinite(iv_arr[0]) else np.nan
    k_arr = np.log(strikes / forward)  # forward moneyness, consistent with surface
    return pd.DataFrame({"tau": tau, "tau_days": tau * ann_days, "strike": strikes, "k": k_arr, "price": prices, "price_se": price_ses, "iv": ivs})

10) Rough Heston and the Fractional Riccati Equation

Rough Heston keeps the Heston-style economic structure but replaces the ordinary mean-reverting variance diffusion with a Volterra fractional process. Instead of:

\[ dV_t = \kappa(\theta - V_t)dt + \sigma_v\sqrt{V_t}dB_t \]

we write variance in integral form:

\[ V_t = V_0 + \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t-s)^{\alpha-1}\kappa(\theta - V_s)\,ds + \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t-s)^{\alpha-1}\sigma_v\sqrt{V_s}\,dB_s \]

where:

\[ \alpha = H + \frac{1}{2} \]

and \(H \in (0,1/2)\) gives \(\alpha \in (1/2,1)\).

The kernel is:

\[ K(t-s) = \frac{(t-s)^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \]

This kernel is singular near \(s=t\) because \(\alpha-1<0\). That singularity is the mathematical reason the process reacts sharply to recent shocks. At the same time, the kernel integrates over the entire past, so the model is non-Markovian.

A useful operator here is the fractional integral:

\[ I^\alpha f(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t-s)^{\alpha-1}f(s)\,ds \]

Rough Heston can be written compactly as:

\[ V_t = V_0 + I^\alpha\left[\kappa(\theta - V_\cdot)\right](t) + I^\alpha\left[\sigma_v\sqrt{V_\cdot}\,dB_\cdot\right](t) \]

This explains why rough Heston is harder than Heston. In Heston, the next variance step depends on the current variance. In rough Heston, the current variance contains a weighted memory of the whole past path through a fractional kernel.

The payoff is that rough Heston remains semi-tractable. Its characteristic function has a structure similar to classical Heston, but the ordinary Riccati equation becomes a fractional Riccati equation.

10.1 Memory Kernel Shape

The kernel:

\[ K(t-s)=\frac{(t-s)^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \]

has two important properties.

First, near \(s=t\), the term \((t-s)^{\alpha-1}\) becomes large because \(\alpha-1<0\). This means very recent shocks receive a large weight. That creates rough local behavior.

Second, the kernel decays slowly enough that older shocks are not forgotten with a simple exponential half-life. This creates a fractional memory structure.

Compare this with ordinary Heston. Ordinary Heston mean reversion creates exponential memory:

\[ e^{-\kappa(t-s)} \]

A shock decays at a rate controlled by \(\kappa\). In rough Heston, the memory shape is power-law-like rather than exponential. The volatility path can be locally jagged and still remember past shocks in a non-exponential way.

This is a big modeling difference. Exponential memory is easy to update recursively. Fractional memory requires keeping track of a weighted history or approximating it through numerical schemes. The pricing engine has to pay that cost through the fractional Riccati solver.

10.2 Fractional Riccati Structure

In classical Heston, Fourier pricing depends on a Riccati equation. Rough Heston keeps the same spirit but changes the derivative operator. In simplified form, the Riccati function \(\psi(t,u)\) satisfies:

\[ D^\alpha \psi(t,u) = F(u,\psi(t,u)) \]

where \(D^\alpha\) is a fractional derivative and \(u\) is the Fourier variable. The function \(F\) has the same economic ingredients as Heston:

\[ F(u,\psi) = \frac{1}{2}(u^2 + iu) + (\rho\sigma_v iu - \kappa)\psi + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_v^2\psi^2 \]

Different sign conventions appear depending on whether the characteristic function is written for \(X_t=\log S_t\) or for shifted Fourier variables, but the structure is the key:

  • the constant term comes from return variance exposure,
  • the linear term contains mean reversion and spot-vol correlation,
  • the quadratic term comes from volatility-of-volatility.

The fractional derivative means \(\psi(t,u)\) at time \(t\) is not updated like an ordinary ODE step. It depends on a memory convolution. Numerically, we solve it on a grid. If the grid has \(N\) time steps, each new step needs weighted contributions from previous steps. So we check Riccati convergence across different step counts.

The pricing engine then uses the characteristic function inside the Fourier/COS machinery from Project 14. So the full rough-Heston pricing pipeline is:

  1. choose parameters \((H,V_0,\kappa,\theta,\sigma_v,\rho)\),
  2. solve the fractional Riccati equation for each maturity and Fourier node,
  3. build the characteristic function,
  4. price options with Fourier/COS integration,
  5. compare model prices with market prices,
  6. calibrate parameters to minimize weighted pricing errors.

The rough part enters through step 2. Everything after that looks familiar from characteristic-function pricing, but the characteristic function is much more expensive to obtain.

10.3 From Fractional Riccati to Option Prices

The fractional Riccati equation is the bridge between rough Heston dynamics and option prices. The model doesn’t simulate every variance path for pricing. Instead, it computes the characteristic function of log-price and then prices options with the Fourier machinery from Project 14.

Let:

\[ X_T=\log S_T \]

The characteristic function is:

\[ \phi_T(u)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{iuX_T}\right] \]

Once we know \(\phi_T(u)\), we can recover option prices through Fourier inversion or COS expansion. That is why rough Heston is powerful: even though the variance process is non-Markovian, the model still has a semi-analytical route to prices.

The rough part enters through the Riccati function \(\psi(t,u)\). In an ordinary Heston model, \(\psi\) solves a standard ODE. In rough Heston, it solves a fractional equation. The numerical solver builds \(\psi\) on a time grid:

\[ 0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_N=T \]

At each time step, the value of \(\psi(t_n,u)\) depends on a weighted sum of previous values. The weights come from the fractional kernel. A rough way to write the discretized memory is:

\[ \psi_n \approx \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} a_{n-j}F(u,\psi_j) \]

where the weights \(a_{n-j}\) decay like a power law rather than exponentially. Recent values get large weights because the kernel is singular near the present, but older values still matter. That is the numerical signature of rough volatility.

The model price then follows this chain:

  1. choose rough-Heston parameters,
  2. solve the fractional Riccati equation for each Fourier frequency \(u\),
  3. assemble the characteristic function \(\phi_T(u)\),
  4. pass \(\phi_T(u)\) into the Fourier/COS pricing formula,
  5. compare the model price to market prices.

The convergence diagnostic checks whether this chain is stable when we increase the Riccati grid resolution. If prices change materially between 512 and 1024 steps, the calibration result can’t be trusted because the objective is reacting to numerical error. The SPX convergence table shows maximum price differences around a few cents across maturities in the test grid, which is reasonable for the simplified research engine.

There is also a practical reason we keep this diagnostic close to the model fit. Rough volatility models can look impressive because they add advanced mathematics, but the numerical layer can quietly dominate the result. For example, a bad Fourier truncation range can make a parameter set look worse than it is. A coarse fractional grid can make short-maturity prices unstable. These convergence checks make the pricing engine part of the research result instead of treating it as a black box.

The fractional Riccati equation is the mathematical center of rough Heston. In classical Heston, the Riccati equation is an ordinary differential equation:

\[ \frac{d\psi}{dt}=F(u,\psi) \]

A numerical solver only needs the current value of \(\psi\) to step forward. In rough Heston, we replace the ordinary derivative with a fractional derivative:

\[ D^\alpha\psi=F(u,\psi) \]

This means the current update depends on a weighted history of previous \(\psi\) values. A grid solver is effectively doing something like:

\[ \psi_n \approx \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} a_{n,j}F(u,\psi_j) \]

where the weights \(a_{n,j}\) come from the fractional kernel. The exact scheme can vary, but the interpretation is stable: every new Riccati value carries memory.

This is why convergence checks across 128, 256, 512, and 1024 steps matter. If the fractional Riccati solution changes too much as the grid is refined, the resulting option prices cannot be trusted. The convergence diagnostics are not optional polish; they are part of making rough-Heston pricing credible.

def fractional_riccati_adams_numba(u, alpha, kappa, sigma_v, rho, tau, n_steps, gamma_alpha_1, gamma_alpha_2, gamma_beta_1):
    m = max(1, int(n_steps))
    n_u = u.size
    dt = tau / m
    dt_alpha = dt ** alpha
    psi = np.zeros((m + 1, n_u), dtype=np.complex128)
    f = np.zeros((m + 1, n_u), dtype=np.complex128)
    predictor_weights = np.empty(m, dtype=np.float64)
    for j in range(1, m + 1):
        predictor_weights[j - 1] = (j ** alpha - (j - 1.0) ** alpha) * dt_alpha / gamma_alpha_1
    for col in range(n_u):
        uu = u[col]
        f[0, col] = -0.5 * (uu * uu + 1j * uu)
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        n = i - 1
        a0 = n ** (alpha + 1.0) - (n - alpha) * (n + 1.0) ** alpha
        for col in range(n_u):
            uu = u[col]
            pred = 0.0 + 0.0j
            for j in range(i):
                pred += predictor_weights[i - 1 - j] * f[j, col]
            f_pred = -0.5 * (uu * uu + 1j * uu) + (1j * uu * rho * sigma_v - kappa) * pred + 0.5 * sigma_v * sigma_v * pred * pred
            corr = a0 * f[0, col]
            for j in range(1, i):
                jj = float(j)
                weight = (n - jj + 2.0) ** (alpha + 1.0) + (n - jj) ** (alpha + 1.0) - 2.0 * (n - jj + 1.0) ** (alpha + 1.0)
                corr += weight * f[j, col]
            psi[i, col] = dt_alpha * (corr + f_pred) / gamma_alpha_2
            val = psi[i, col]
            f[i, col] = -0.5 * (uu * uu + 1j * uu) + (1j * uu * rho * sigma_v - kappa) * val + 0.5 * sigma_v * sigma_v * val * val
    integral = np.empty(n_u, dtype=np.complex128)
    for col in range(n_u):
        total = 0.5 * (psi[0, col] + psi[m, col])
        for j in range(1, m):
            total += psi[j, col]
        integral[col] = dt * total
    beta = 1.0 - alpha
    fractional_terminal = np.empty(n_u, dtype=np.complex128)
    if beta <= 1e-10:
        for col in range(n_u):
            fractional_terminal[col] = psi[m, col]
    else:
        dt_beta = dt ** beta
        for col in range(n_u):
            total = 0.0 + 0.0j
            for j in range(1, m + 1):
                weight = ((m - j + 1.0) ** beta - (m - j) ** beta) * dt_beta / gamma_beta_1
                total += weight * psi[j, col]
            fractional_terminal[col] = total
    return psi, integral, fractional_terminal


def riccati_terms(u, params, tau, n_steps):

    alpha = np.clip(h, 1e-6, 0.5) + 0.5
    m = max(1, int(n_steps))
    tau = max(float(tau), 1e-12)
    dt = tau / m
    t = np.linspace(0.0, tau, m + 1)

    gamma_alpha_2 = math.gamma(alpha + 2.0)
    gamma_beta_1 = math.gamma(max(1.0 - alpha, 0.0) + 1.0)
    try:
        psi, integral, fractional_terminal = fractional_riccati_adams_numba(
            u,
            float(alpha),
            float(kappa),
            float(sigma_v),
            float(rho),

            float(gamma_alpha_2),
            float(gamma_beta_1),
        )
        return t, psi, integral, fractional_terminal, alpha
    except Exception:
        pass
    psi = np.zeros((m + 1, len(u)), dtype=complex)
    f = np.zeros_like(psi)
    f[0] = riccati_rhs(u, psi[0], kappa, sigma_v, rho)
    j = np.arange(1, m + 1, dtype=float)
    predictor_weights = (j ** alpha - (j - 1.0) ** alpha) * dt ** alpha / gamma_alpha_1
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        pred = predictor_weights[:i][::-1] @ f[:i]
        f_pred = riccati_rhs(u, pred, kappa, sigma_v, rho)
        n = i - 1

11) Rough-Heston Calibration

The rough-Heston calibration uses the same option target grid idea as the Heston calibration, but the parameter vector expands to:

\[ \Theta_{\text{rough}} = (H,V_0,\kappa,\theta,\sigma_v,\rho) \]

In this implementation, \(H\) is often anchored or constrained by the skew-estimation step rather than freely optimized every time. This is a practical choice. If \(H\) is allowed to float freely with a small number of quotes and limited function evaluations, the optimizer can use it as a noise-fitting parameter. Anchoring \(H\) to the skew power law keeps the model tied to the roughness evidence.

The objective is still a weighted pricing error:

\[ \min_{\Theta_{\text{rough}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\left(C_i^{\text{rough}}(\Theta_{\text{rough}})-C_i^{\text{market}}\right)^2 \]

but rough Heston has extra numerical layers:

  • fractional Riccati grid size,
  • Fourier/COS truncation and term count,
  • parameter bounds,
  • skew penalty if the model-implied short-end skew moves too far from the surface-implied roughness,
  • finite-price and numerical stability filters.

The code also builds bucket diagnostics by moneyness and maturity:

\[ \text{scaled residual}_i = \frac{C_i^{\text{model}}-C_i^{\text{market}}}{\text{calibration scale}_i} \]

Then it groups residuals into buckets such as deep OTM puts, near-ATM options, short maturities, and medium maturities. This is more informative than a single global RMSE because rough models are mainly supposed to help in specific regions: short maturities and skew-sensitive downside strikes.

11.1 Skew Penalty and Fixed-H Design

Rough-Heston calibration can become unstable if \(H\) freely absorbs every small residual. We use a more controlled design. The roughness parameter is guided by the skew estimate, and the calibration can include a skew penalty.

A simplified objective looks like:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\Theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\left(C_i^{\text{model}}(\Theta)-C_i^{\text{market}}\right)^2 + \lambda_{\text{skew}}\left(\psi_{\text{model}}-\psi_{\text{surface}}\right)^2 \]

The first part fits prices. The second part keeps the model’s short-end skew behavior close to the surface-implied skew. This is useful because the model could otherwise improve price fit in one region while damaging the roughness signature that motivated the model.

The fixed-\(H\) or skew-anchored approach is also a good research design. It separates two questions:

  1. Is the market showing roughness through realized variance and ATM skew?
  2. Given that roughness, does the rough model improve pricing or Greeks?

If we optimize \(H\) freely, these questions become mixed together. A free optimizer may find an \(H\) that improves price fit but has weak roughness interpretation. Anchoring \(H\) keeps the model more honest.

Reading the Rough-Heston Diagnostics

The rough-Heston comparison shouldn’t be reduced to one RMSE number. A single weighted RMSE is useful, but rough volatility has a more specific purpose: it should help with the short-end skew and the regions where Markovian Heston struggles.

That is why we build bucket diagnostics. Each quote is grouped by two dimensions:

  • moneyness bucket, based on log-moneyness \(k=\log(K/F)\),
  • maturity bucket, based on time to expiry \(\tau\).

The residual is scaled first:

\[ \varepsilon_i^{\text{scaled}}=\frac{C_i^{\text{model}}-C_i^{\text{market}}}{s_i} \]

The scale \(s_i\) prevents expensive high-delta options from dominating all diagnostics. A raw dollar error of \(1\) has a different meaning for a deep ITM option than for a cheap OTM put. Scaling makes the comparison closer to a relative pricing-quality measure.

For each bucket, the improvement is:

\[ \text{Improvement}_{b} = 1-\frac{\text{RMSE}_{b,\text{rough}}}{\text{RMSE}_{b,\text{Heston}}} \]

A positive value means rough Heston reduces the bucket error. A negative value means ordinary Heston fits that bucket better. This is exactly the kind of diagnostic we need because a rough model can improve the downside skew while worsening near-ATM medium maturity fit.

The SPX common-date table shows Heston winning the global metrics: lower weighted price RMSE, lower weighted IV RMSE, better bid-ask hit rate, lower OTM put RMSE, and faster runtime. That would make Heston look clearly better if we only cared about global pricing loss. But the bucket table shows rough Heston has strong positive improvements in some skew-sensitive zones, including several OTM/short-to-medium maturity buckets.

This is a good result for the project because it is honest. Rough Heston is not presented as a magic replacement for Heston. We see exactly where it helps and where it costs. The model improves parts of the surface connected to rough skew behavior, but it is slower and not globally better on this dataset. That is a much more realistic research conclusion than claiming the most advanced model always wins.

There is another important interpretation. The rough-Heston calibration in we use limited dates, limited iterations, fixed or anchored \(H\), and a teaching-oriented engine. A production calibration desk would tune optimizer restarts, parameter transformations, weighting schemes, time grids, and the Fourier/COS settings much more aggressively. So the result should be read as a controlled research comparison, not as a final claim that one model dominates across all calibration choices.

12) Model-Implied ATM Skew

After calibration, we compare market, Heston, and rough-Heston ATM skew term structures directly. This is one of the cleanest tests in the whole project because rough volatility is mainly motivated by the short-end skew power law.

For each model, we compute prices at three nearby log-moneyness nodes:

\[ k \in \{-\Delta k, 0, +\Delta k\} \]

convert those model prices back into implied volatilities, and estimate:

\[ \psi_{\text{model}}(\tau) \approx \frac{\sigma_{\text{model}}(+\Delta k,\tau)-\sigma_{\text{model}}(-\Delta k,\tau)}{2\Delta k} \]

Then we fit the same power law:

\[ |\psi_{\text{model}}(\tau)| \approx c_{\text{model}}\tau^{-\alpha_{\text{model}}} \]

with:

\[ H_{\text{model}} = \frac{1}{2} - \alpha_{\text{model}} \]

We use this mainly as a diagnostic. It shows whether the model reproduces the slope behavior that motivated rough volatility.

The selected market date has a market-implied short-end skew fit with \(R^2\) around 0.993 and \(H\) around 0.258 from the skew window. Heston also produces a declining skew term structure, but its implied power-law estimate is different. Rough Heston produces an even steeper short-end skew curve in this selected diagnostic, with a fitted exponent that can overshoot the market.

That overshoot is useful to see. Rough models can generate steep short-end skew, but calibration still has to control how steep. Adding roughness creates the right kind of shape, but it doesn’t automatically guarantee the right magnitude.

12.1 Market Skew, Model Skew, and Calibration Tension

A model can fit option prices well and still produce the wrong skew power law. The reason is that price errors are weighted across many strikes and maturities, while the skew power law focuses on a derivative at \(k=0\) and short maturities.

The market skew diagnostic uses:

\[ \psi_{\text{market}}(\tau) = \partial_k\sigma_{\text{surface}}(0,\tau) \]

The model skew diagnostic uses:

\[ \psi_{\text{model}}(\tau) = \partial_k\sigma_{\text{model}}(0,\tau) \]

where \(\sigma_{\text{model}}\) is obtained by repricing model options and inverting them back to implied volatility.

This model-to-IV inversion step matters. We don’t compare model variance parameters directly with market skew. We compare the implied-volatility surface generated by the model with the market surface. That keeps the diagnostic in the same units as option traders use.

If rough Heston generates a too-steep short-end skew, the fitted \(H_{\text{model}}\) from the power law can become lower or even unstable. If it generates a too-flat skew, \(H_{\text{model}}\) becomes too high. The goal isn’t only to make \(H\) small; it is to match the observed maturity decay of skew.

13) Delta Under Rough Volatility

The last SPX modeling layer asks how rough volatility changes hedge sensitivities. We already covered Greeks in Project 4 and surface-aware Greeks in Project 8, so here we focus on the comparison.

Delta is:

\[ \Delta = \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \]

For model prices that don’t have a simple closed-form delta, we use a finite difference:

\[ \Delta \approx \frac{C(S+\Delta S)-C(S-\Delta S)}{2\Delta S} \]

with:

\[ \Delta S = bS \]

where \(b\) is the bump size. The code compares four delta notions:

  • flat BSM delta using one ATM volatility,
  • surface BSM delta using the local implied volatility at each \((k,\tau)\),
  • Heston delta from finite differences,
  • rough-Heston delta from finite differences.

This matters because the delta of an option in a skewed stochastic-volatility model is not just the BSM delta with a better volatility number. When spot moves, the whole variance distribution and smile can move too. Rough volatility affects this interaction because variance shocks are more reactive near the present.

For an equity index with negative spot-vol correlation, downside moves usually come with higher variance. That makes downside option values more sensitive to spot than flat BSM suggests. The finite-difference model delta captures this because the model reprices the option under bumped spot levels.

13.1 Delta with Smile Feedback

Flat BSM delta treats volatility as constant:

\[ \Delta_{\text{flat}} = \frac{\partial C(S,K,\tau,\sigma)}{\partial S} \]

Surface-BSM delta uses the implied volatility at each strike and maturity, but it still treats the surface mostly as an input map:

\[ \Delta_{\text{surface}} \approx \frac{\partial C(S,K,\tau,\sigma_{\text{imp}}(k,\tau))}{\partial S} \]

A stochastic-volatility model delta is different because the model links spot and volatility through \(\rho\) and through the variance process. When spot moves, the model-implied distribution changes. For rough Heston:

\[ \Delta_{\text{rough}} \approx \frac{C_{\text{rough}}(S+\Delta S)-C_{\text{rough}}(S-\Delta S)}{2\Delta S} \]

The two bumped prices use the full rough-Heston pricing model, not just a BSM formula with a local IV. This captures the model’s belief about how spot and variance interact.

For downside equity puts and near-ATM short-dated calls, this can matter a lot. A negative spot shock increases the probability of higher variance in leverage-effect models. That changes the option’s value beyond the direct intrinsic-value effect. Roughness can amplify the short-horizon version of this effect.

The delta table shows that rough-Heston deltas are often higher than flat BSM and surface-BSM deltas around ATM and slightly downside moneyness. For example, at 30 days and near ATM, flat BSM delta is around 0.508, surface-BSM delta is around 0.508, while rough-Heston delta is around 0.591. That is a large difference for hedge sizing.

The heatmaps show where the difference is strongest. Rough-Heston minus surface-BSM delta is positive across much of the tested grid, especially on the downside and around short to medium maturities. Rough-Heston minus Heston is smaller and can change sign depending on moneyness, which means the stochastic-volatility effect is already present in Heston, while the roughness component adds a more specific adjustment.

This is one of the practical reasons rough volatility matters. Even if a rough model doesn’t win every calibration metric, it can change Greeks in regions where hedging is most fragile. If a trader hedges a short-dated skew position with flat-BSM delta, the hedge can be materially under-sized.

A practical hedge example makes the difference concrete. Suppose a trader is short 1,000 near-ATM 30-day SPX calls. If flat/surface BSM delta is around \(0.508\) but rough-Heston delta is around \(0.591\), the hedge difference is:

\[ 1000 \times (0.591-0.508) \approx 83 \]

That is about 83 SPX units of delta exposure. On an index level near 3,925 in the selected example, this is not a small discrepancy. The direction also matters: the rough model says the position is more spot-sensitive than the surface-BSM calculation suggests.

This is one reason advanced models can be useful even when their calibration RMSE is not the lowest. A pricing model can affect risk management through Greeks and hedge ratios, not only through the quoted model price.

Reading the SPX Evidence as One Workflow

The dashboard is not just a summary figure. It links four separate kinds of evidence:

  1. historical roughness from realized-variance scaling,
  2. forecast usefulness from rough-kernel variance forecasts,
  3. option-implied roughness from ATM skew power laws,
  4. pricing and hedging consequences from rough models.

The strongest result is not one single table. It is the alignment between several diagnostics. The historical realized-variance side says volatility is locally rough after controlling for proxy issues. The option side says short-end skew follows a power law. The rough models can generate steep short-end skew and materially different deltas. The calibration comparison shows this comes with costs and doesn’t automatically dominate Heston.

That is a good research outcome. It teaches the model’s purpose and its limitations at the same time.

Connecting the SPX Evidence

The SPX dashboard is useful because it puts three different roughness measurements next to each other:

\[ H_{\text{RV}}, \qquad H_{\text{skew}}, \qquad H_{\text{model}} \]

These are related, but they are estimated from different objects.

\(H_{\text{RV}}\) comes from historical realized variance. It is a physical-measure estimate because it uses actual realized returns. The raw daily squared-return proxy gives almost no reliable scaling structure because it is too noisy. The rolling proxies are smoother and produce larger \(H\) values, but overlap and smoothing can push estimates upward. The block/non-overlapping checks help reduce that bias.

\(H_{\text{skew}}\) comes from option prices. It is risk-neutral because it uses the implied volatility surface. The short-end skew power law is one of the most important rough-volatility diagnostics:

\[ |\psi(\tau)| \approx c\tau^{H-\frac{1}{2}} \]

If the estimated slope is close to \(-\frac{1}{2}\), then \(H\) is close to zero and the skew explodes very sharply as maturity gets short. If the slope is less steep, \(H\) is larger. The SPX high-quality skew estimate around \(H\approx0.36\) to \(0.41\) is not as rough as the original \(H\approx0.1\) empirical result from high-frequency realized variance literature, but it still points to rougher-than-Brownian implied volatility because it is below \(0.5\).

\(H_{\text{model}}\) comes from rough-Heston calibration. It is constrained by the model’s ability to fit prices, not only by a slope regression. That makes it a hybrid diagnostic. It tells us what roughness level the model can support while fitting the surface under the chosen parameter bounds and numerical engine.

The dashboard also shows why rough volatility is a full workflow rather than one test. If we only look at realized variance, the answer depends strongly on proxy construction. If we only look at option skew, the answer depends on surface quality and maturity filtering. If we only look at model calibration, the answer depends on optimizer and numerical choices. Putting all three together gives a more stable interpretation:

  • realized variance shows evidence of non-smooth volatility dynamics,
  • option skew shows a short-end power-law structure,
  • rough Heston can reproduce parts of that skew structure,
  • the pricing comparison still has to be checked bucket by bucket,
  • deltas change because the model changes the spot-volatility interaction.

This is also why the project belongs after Projects 4, 8, 11, 13, and 14. We already know how to extract IV, build surfaces, price under stochastic volatility, and use Fourier/COS methods. Here we use all of that machinery to ask a sharper question: whether volatility itself behaves like a rough process and whether that roughness matters for pricing and hedging.

The most useful practical reading is:

  1. SPX volatility is rough in several diagnostics, especially when we avoid trusting only a smoothed overlapping RV proxy.
  2. Option skew carries a clear roughness signature, because ATM skew follows a power-law shape across short maturities on many dates.
  3. rBergomi is conceptually clean for rough smile generation, but Monte Carlo calibration is noisy and expensive.
  4. rough Heston is more production-friendly than rBergomi, because it can use characteristic-function pricing, but fractional Riccati computation is still heavier than classical Heston.
  5. rough models change hedge sensitivities, especially near ATM and downside short-maturity options.

This gives the project its place in the series. Project 11 compared stochastic-volatility and jump models. Project 14 built the Fourier machinery. Here we use that machinery to test a deeper question: whether the time regularity of volatility itself should be part of the model.

15) BTC Library Implementation

The final cell repeats the workflow on BTC using the packaged library. This secondary application is important because BTC has a very different volatility ecosystem from SPX:

  • spot volatility is much higher,
  • option quotes are noisier,
  • moneyness and maturity support can be uneven,
  • crypto options are strongly shaped by leverage, funding, liquidations, and weekend trading,
  • BTC trades continuously, so the annualization basis uses 365 days rather than 252 trading days.

The data comes from:

  • btc_yfinance for BTC spot,
  • btc_optionsdx for BTC/Deribit options,
  • us_treasury_yields for discounting.

The BTC code also performs a unit audit because Deribit-style option prices can be quoted in BTC units while the spot is in USD. If the option mid is in BTC and BTC spot is around 64,000, a raw mid of \(0.05\) BTC corresponds to several thousand dollars:

\[ C_{\text{USD}} = C_{\text{BTC}} \times S_{\text{BTCUSD}} \]

The audit table confirms this conversion: the median raw mid is about 0.0546 BTC, and the USD-equivalent median mid is about 3,369 USD. This step is critical. Without it, the calibration would compare option prices and spot levels in inconsistent units.

15.1 Continuous-Time Market Structure

BTC has a different time structure from SPX, not only a higher volatility level. SPX cash trading is tied to exchange hours, while BTC trades continuously. That changes the annualization convention and the meaning of weekend risk.

We use:

\[ \text{annualization}_{BTC}=365 \]

instead of:

\[ \text{annualization}_{SPX}=252 \]

This affects realized variance, forecast scaling, and option maturity conversion. A 7-day BTC option really contains 7 calendar days of trading. A 7-calendar-day SPX option contains fewer cash-market trading sessions, though options still price overnight and weekend risk indirectly.

This is one reason BTC roughness and BTC skew need separate interpretation. The time clock is different, the market microstructure is different, and the volatility risk premium is shaped by different participants.

A model can handle the variance scale well under QLIKE while still having imperfect timing and low volatility correlation. Crypto volatility is less stable than SPX volatility, so a strong variance-loss result doesn’t mean the forecast path is visually perfect.

The BTC surface has a much larger option panel after cleaning: about 121k clean option rows and 120k surface-ready rows. The selected main date is 2024-01-13, chosen by a high-quality short-skew rule. The short-skew fit gives:

\[ H_{\text{skew}} \approx 0.121 \]

with \(R^2\) close to 0.999, which is a very clean power-law fit for the selected date.

This is interesting because the realized-variance roughness side uses a clipped \(H\) around 0.35, while the option-skew side says the short-end implied skew behaves much rougher, around 0.12. For BTC, the option market’s short-end smile can be extremely steep even when historical realized variance scaling is less rough. That difference likely comes from jump risk, liquidity, crash-up/crash-down asymmetry, and the way crypto options price weekend and liquidation risk.

The BTC Heston versus rough-Heston comparison is more favorable to rough Heston than the SPX aggregate table. Rough Heston has lower weighted price RMSE (1.745 vs 1.783), lower weighted IV RMSE (0.0498 vs 0.0544), lower OTM put RMSE, lower short-maturity RMSE, and a slightly better bid-ask hit rate. The rBergomi calibration for BTC picks \(H \approx 0.121\), \(\nu \approx 0.8\), and \(\rho \approx -0.3\), with a high IV RMSE around 0.222. That is not surprising. BTC smiles are noisy, jumpy, and difficult to fit with a small three-parameter rBergomi layer on top of a forward variance curve. The model is still useful as a rough-smile generator, but the calibration quality is not as clean as an ideal textbook case.

The BTC skew-history table also shows extreme instability. Some dates produce impossible or economically weird \(H\) estimates, including negative values or values above 1, even with high \(R^2\) inside a narrow window. That can happen because a power law can fit a small number of maturity points very well while the fitted exponent is not economically plausible.

This is why the implementation filters and clips:

\[ H \in [0.04, 0.45] \]

for rough-Heston use. The filter is not hiding the data. It is preventing noisy option-surface dates from turning the roughness parameter into nonsense. For SPX, high-quality skew dates are more stable. For BTC, quote quality and surface shape vary more, so robust selection is more important.

This is also why the selected BTC main date is chosen with a quality rule: high short-skew \(R^2\) and surface RMSE tie-breaks. The selected date is a teaching date where the rough-volatility signature is clean enough to inspect.

BTC Interpretation Across the Whole Pipeline

BTC is a stronger stress test for the workflow than SPX because the same mathematical model meets a very different market structure.

SPX options are tied to an equity index with deep liquidity, clear macro hedging demand, dealer inventory effects, and a long history of downside crash protection. BTC options are tied to a 24/7 asset with stronger jumps, exchange-specific liquidity, weekend risk, funding and leverage cycles, and a much shorter institutional history. The rough-volatility machinery can still be used, but the interpretation needs more care.

The BTC realized-variance scaling estimates show relatively high \(H\) values for lower moments and lower \(H\) values for higher moments. That pattern means the scaling is not perfectly monofractal. Higher moments are more sensitive to extreme moves, and BTC has many more large jumps than SPX. When the \(q=3\) estimate is much lower than the \(q=0.5\) estimate, the process is telling us that large shocks scale differently from ordinary fluctuations.

The forecast comparison also has a crypto-specific interpretation. The rough kernel wins QLIKE across the visible horizons, which means it forecasts the variance level in a way that is especially good under a likelihood-style loss. HAR is still competitive in MAE and correlation because it captures slower realized-volatility persistence. So BTC volatility has both ingredients:

  • short-memory reaction to shocks, which helps rough kernels,
  • slower regime persistence, which helps HAR-style averages.

The option side is more extreme. The selected BTC skew date gives \(H_{\text{skew}}\approx0.121\) with a very high fit quality. That tells us the short-maturity implied skew decays with maturity very quickly. In simple words, the BTC option market prices very sharp near-term asymmetry. This may come from liquidation cascades, weekend gap risk, event risk around ETFs/regulation, and the fact that crypto traders use options as leveraged crash and breakout exposure.

The model table confirms that rough Heston helps more for BTC than it did globally for SPX. Rough Heston improves weighted price RMSE, weighted IV RMSE, OTM put RMSE, short-maturity RMSE, median absolute price error, and bid-ask hit rate. The cost is runtime: rough Heston is more than ten times slower. This is a real tradeoff. For a research project, the extra runtime is acceptable because we care about understanding the model. For production, this would require caching, parallelization, better parameter warm starts, or a faster compiled engine.

The BTC delta comparison is also different from SPX. In SPX, negative spot-vol correlation creates a strong downside-skew hedge effect. In BTC, skew can be directionally unstable: sometimes downside protection is expensive, sometimes upside calls become extremely expensive during speculative rallies. So rough-Heston delta gaps are present, but they are less one-directional. The model changes the hedge map most where maturity is short and smile curvature is steep.

The final interpretation is that rough volatility transfers well as a modeling language, but it doesn’t transfer as a one-size-fits-all explanation. For SPX, rough volatility is mostly about equity-index downside skew, variance risk premium, and short-end implied-vol behavior. For BTC, roughness is mixed with jumps, liquidity, leverage, and quote-unit issues. The model is still useful because it gives a disciplined way to measure those effects through \(H\), skew power laws, forecast losses, pricing residuals, and hedge deltas.
